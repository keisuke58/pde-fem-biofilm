\chapter{Conclusion}
\label{ch:conclusion}

% Rewritten 3 Oct 2026 for the structure in thesis/README.md. Numbers as in
% chapters 3 and 4.

The task of this thesis was to bring a point model of a multi-species biofilm
into an existing finite element framework, so that its composition, its growth
and the resulting stresses can be computed in space. Three contributions serve
this task.

\paragraph{A calibrated point model (Chapter~\ref{ch:heine}).}
The 15 interaction coefficients of a five-species point model were estimated
with TMCMC from the in-vitro time courses of Heine et al.\ under four
conditions. Compiled with JAX, the forward model runs about 150 times faster
on the GPU than on the CPU, which makes posteriors with $10{,}000$ samples routine. The
inferred matrices separate the commensal from the dysbiotic conditions and
predict a quantity that never entered the calibration: the pH of the flow
reactor, with $R^2=0.78$. With a metabolic sign prior, the same type of model
also predicts sparse in-vivo data of ten patients out of sample.

\paragraph{A growth law in the finite element framework (Chapter~\ref{ch:ansys}).}
The growth kinematics $\bF=\bF_e\bF_g$, $\bF_g=\alpha\bI$, of Klempt et
al.\ 2024 was implemented as an ANSYS user material and verified against
closed-form solutions. It was coupled to the biofilm field of the IKM element
through the growth equation $\dot\alpha=k_\alpha\phi$ at every Gauss point.
Inside ANSYS, the coupled run follows the exact solution of a gradient-free
region to $3\times10^{-11}$ in $\alpha$. With the published parameters the
growth in the seeded element reaches $\alpha-1=8.9\times10^{-4}$ at $T^*=1.1$
and the seeded region is compressed; a finer model of the same problem
predicts, on average, tension in the surrounding material, consistent with the
ring of tension reported by Klempt et al.~\cite{Klempt2024DiffusionDrivenGrowth}.

\paragraph{The composition at every Gauss point (Chapter~\ref{ch:ansys}).}
For two species, the point model of Klempt et al.\ 2026 runs at every Gauss
point alongside the field: the field sets the amount, the point model the
composition. Every call from ANSYS to the point model is reproduced in Python
bit for bit, and the coupled run keeps the type of outcome of the published
cases: coexistence in case~3 and one surviving species in case~6.

Together these steps connect a calibrated description of what a biofilm
consists of with a finite element description of how it grows and what
stresses it carries, for one and two species.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:concl_discussion}

\paragraph{Where the species live.} Table~\ref{tab:concl_models} places this
work among the continuum models it builds on. Feng et
al.~\cite{Feng2021SymbioticBiofilm} and Soleimani et
al.~\cite{Soleimani2023CoAggregation} give every species its own field and
compute no growth stresses; Klempt et al.~\cite{Klempt2024DiffusionDrivenGrowth}
compute these stresses with a single biofilm variable. This work keeps the
single field for the amount of biofilm and its stresses, and adds the
composition as a local state at each Gauss point.

species, and the point model, calibrated on its own, enters unchanged. The
price is that the species cannot move relative to each other: the
composition varies in space only through the local nutrient and, in the Python
study, through the share-weighted front rate. Segregation of species by
different spreading is outside what this scheme can show.

\paragraph{Calibrated and chosen parameters.} The interaction coefficients of
Chapter~\ref{ch:heine} come from measured time courses, and the inferred
matrices predict a quantity that did not enter the calibration, the pH
($R^2=0.78$). The coupled examples of Chapter~\ref{ch:ansys} use the two-species
cases of Klempt et al.~\cite{Klempt2026ContinuumBacterialGrowth}, chosen there
to show coexistence and the survival of one species. Calibrated point-model
parameters and the finite element field have not yet met in one computation;
this needs the five-species state in the element (Outlook).

\paragraph{What the stresses mean.} With the stiffness of Klempt et
al.~\cite{Klempt2024DiffusionDrivenGrowth} the stresses are of order
$10^{-4}$~Pa and scale with the stiffness. What carries over is their pattern:
the seeded region is compressed and the surrounding material is under tension,
as in the comparison of growth models by Klempt et
al.~\cite{Klempt2023NumericalComparison} and in the ring of tension of the 2024
paper. Statements about the magnitude in an oral biofilm need a measured
stiffness and a measured stress, which were not available.

\paragraph{The numerical scheme.} The field of the element is advanced
explicitly. The condition stated by Rudolf et al.~\cite{Rudolf2025NEM} is the
one-dimensional one; on the lattice of the integration points the stability
limit of the NEM stencil follows from a Bloch analysis
(Appendix~\ref{app:stability}) and depends on the mesh through the fixed width
of the weight, from about $0.06$ on $8^3$ to $0.18$ on fine meshes, which the
runs confirm. The update is first order in time. For the seed
problem the stress converges with the mesh at an observed order of about two.
An implicit treatment of the field would remove the step limit; whether it is
also more accurate for the same cost is the comparison planned at Keio.

\paragraph{Symmetric interactions.} The variational derivation makes the
interaction matrix symmetric. A symmetric matrix cannot describe one species
feeding another without the reverse, which the metabolic literature reports for
several of the pairs. In this work the symmetric model reproduces the
in-vitro time courses and predicts the pH; whether a directed term is needed
for in-vivo data is open.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:concl_limitations}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Verification only.} The finite element results are
        verified against closed forms and against the published models. No
        measured stress of a growing oral biofilm was available to validate
        them.
  \item \emph{One-way coupling.} The composition does not act on the
        stiffness, the spreading or the stress, following Klempt et al.\ 2024,
        who drop the mechanical term in the growth equation.
  \item \emph{Spreading in ANSYS.} The front term of the element is inactive,
        so the biofilm spreads only by diffusion in the ANSYS runs. The
        composition varies there through the local nutrient, but not through
        growth towards the nutrient.
  \item \emph{Assumptions.} The link between the time of the point model and
        that of the field, and the cap on the amount handed to the point
        model, are not given by the papers. Both are reported with their
        sensitivity.
  \item \emph{The source model.} The test cases of Klempt et al.\ 2024 are
        reproduced only with stated departures from the printed equations
        (Appendix~\ref{app:klempt2024}), and the paper gives two forms of the
        law for $\alpha$. I follow its Eq.~36.
  \item \emph{Mesh and magnitude.} The example model's mesh overestimates the
        averaged stresses of the seed by a factor of about 1.7; peaks at its
        corners are mesh dependent. On $24^3$ elements the error of the seed
        stress is estimated at about $2\,\%$, from three meshes only. With the published parameters the stresses
        are of order $10^{-4}$~Pa.
  \item \emph{Data.} The calibration rests on five species under four
        conditions in vitro and ten patients in vivo. The commensal and
        dysbiotic consortia used different \textit{P.~gingivalis} strains, so
        part of the difference between the inferred matrices reflects the
        strains. The symmetric interaction matrix cannot represent directed
        effects.
\end{itemize}

\section{Outlook}
\label{sec:concl_outlook}

The work continues at Keio University. The steps follow from the limitations,
in order of how well the literature supports them.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{More species.} The five-species point model calibrated in
        Chapter~\ref{ch:heine} and the bridge from ANSYS to Python already
        exist; with the scheme of Chapter~\ref{ch:ansys} the field stays a
        single variable, so only the state of the point model and its
        parameters need to be widened.
  \item \emph{A working front term.} Once the element spreads the biofilm, the
        composition can vary in space in ANSYS as it does in the Python
        demonstration. Where the nutrient is uniform the direction of the
        front term is undefined; the authors' implementation regularises it
        as $\nabla c/(\|\nabla c\|+\varepsilon)$, which the element can adopt.
  \item \emph{Local nutrient and composition-dependent spreading.} Replacing
        the constant nutrient level $c^*$ of the point model by the local
        nutrient of the field, and letting the growth and consumption of the
        field depend on the composition, would close the loop between the two
        models. First computations in Python
        (Section~\ref{sec:ch5-outlook-python}) show that both change the
        result: with a local nutrient the composition varies across the seed
        (now also in ANSYS), and with species-dependent spreading the amount of biofilm at $T^*=1$
        changes by up to $8\,\%$.
  \item \emph{Composition into stiffness and stress into growth.} Species-specific
        stiffness and stress-dependent growth would let the composition reach
        the stress directly and the stress act back. Both need parameters that
        are not yet available.
  \item \emph{Uncertainty into the stress.} The posterior of
        Chapter~\ref{ch:heine} could be carried through the element to the
        stress; time-separated stochastic mechanics~\cite{GeislerJunker2025TSM}
        does this at a much lower cost than Monte Carlo sampling.
  \item \emph{A test of the inferred network.} The calibrated matrices predict
        that removing \textit{F.~nucleatum} from the dysbiotic consortium
        suppresses the late growth of \textit{P.~gingivalis}; a co-culture
        without it would test this.
\end{enumerate}

This chapter collects the two models that the thesis connects and the
inference method used to calibrate one of them. Both models are derived from
the extended Hamilton principle (Section~\ref{sec:theory_hamilton}). The field
model of Klempt et al.\ 2024 describes a single biofilm phase that grows in
space and carries stress (Section~\ref{sec:theory_field}); the point model of
Klempt et al.\ 2026 describes the composition of several species at one
material point (Section~\ref{sec:theory_point}). Section~\ref{sec:theory_link}
states how the two are combined in this thesis, and
Sections~\ref{sec:theory_tmcmc} and~\ref{sec:theory_fba} cover Bayesian
inference with TMCMC and the metabolic sign prior.

\section{Extended Hamilton Principle}
\label{sec:theory_hamilton}

The extended Hamilton principle~\cite{JunkerBalzani2021ExtendedHamilton}
derives the governing equations of a dissipative material from one stationarity
condition. For a quasi-static, isothermal process it reads, in the form used by
Klempt et al.~\cite{Klempt2024DiffusionDrivenGrowth} (their Eq.~19),
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal H=\int_\tau\bigl(\mathcal G+\mathcal C-\mathcal B+\mathcal D\bigr)\,\mathrm dt
  \;\to\;\operatorname*{stat}_{\mathbf u,\boldsymbol\xi},
  \label{eq:hamilton_principle}
\end{equation}
with the Gibbs energy $\mathcal G$ of the body (stored energy minus the work of
external loads), constraint terms $\mathcal C$, a non-conservative source
$\mathcal B$ (growth), and the dissipation $\mathcal D$. The displacement
$\mathbf u$ and the internal variables $\boldsymbol\xi$ are varied
independently. The dissipation is modelled by a dissipation function, here
rate-dependent and quadratic,
$\Delta^{\rm diss}=\tfrac{\eta}{2}\|\dot{\boldsymbol\xi}\|^2$, whose derivative
$\partial\Delta^{\rm diss}/\partial\dot{\boldsymbol\xi}$ gives the
non-conservative forces. Stationarity with respect to $\mathbf u$ gives the
balance of linear momentum in weak form; stationarity with respect to each
internal variable gives its evolution equation. A model is then fixed by
choosing the energy, the source and the dissipation function.
For the point model of Chapter~\ref{ch:heine}, which has no displacement field,
the principle reduces to its local form, the principle of the minimum of the
dissipation potential~\cite{junker2014principle, hackl1997generalized}:
$\dot\Psi+\Delta+C\to\operatorname{stat}$ with respect to the rates of the
internal variables and the Lagrange multiplier. A recent account of this
variational framework for dissipative materials is given by Junker, Bode and
Hackl~\cite{JunkerBodeHackl2025Holistic}.

\section{The Growth Field of Klempt et al.\ 2024}
\label{sec:theory_field}

\subsection{Variables and kinematics}

Klempt et al.~\cite{Klempt2024DiffusionDrivenGrowth} describe a biofilm growing
into a void that holds a nutrient. The state is given by the displacement
$\mathbf u(\mathbf X,t)$ and three internal variables:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the biofilm state variable $\phi(\mathbf X,t)\in[0,1]$, $1$ in biofilm
        and $0$ in the void;
  \item the nutrient concentration $c(\mathbf X,t)$;
  \item the local expansion $\alpha(\mathbf X,t)$, an internal variable at the
        Gauss points.
\end{itemize}
Growth enters the kinematics through the multiplicative split of the
deformation gradient~\cite{Rodriguez1994Growth},
\begin{equation}
  \bF=\bF_e\bF_g,\qquad \bF_g=\alpha\,\bI,\qquad \alpha(t=0)=1,
  \label{eq:theory_split}
\end{equation}
so that $\alpha-1$ is the isotropic growth strain. The elastic part $\bF_e$
carries the stress. The paper treats the biofilm as incompressible,
$\det\bF_e=1$, enforced by a pressure $p$; in this thesis the constraint is
replaced by a nearly incompressible material with $\nu=0.49$
(Chapter~\ref{ch:ansys}).

\subsection{Energy, growth and consumption}

The energy density (Eq.~20 of the paper) is
\begin{equation}
  \Psi=\phi^2\,\frac{\mu}{2}\bigl(\bI:\mathbf C_e-3\bigr)
       +\frac{\bar\beta}{2}\|\nabla_X\phi\|^2
       +\frac{\bar d}{2}\|\nabla_X c\|^2
       -\bar k_\alpha\,\alpha\,\phi ,
  \label{eq:theory_energy}
\end{equation}
with $\mathbf C_e=\bF_e^{\rm T}\bF_e$. The first term is a neo-Hookean
material whose stiffness is weighted by $\phi^2$, so the void carries no
stiffness. The two gradient terms regularise $\phi$ and $c$, and the last term
links the expansion $\alpha$ to the biofilm.

The growth source spreads the biofilm towards the nutrient. It is the product of
a Monod function and an orientation function (Eqs.~21--23),
\begin{equation}
  h^*_\phi=\tilde r(c)\,o(\nabla_X\phi,\nabla_X c),\qquad
  \tilde r(c)=\|\nabla_X\phi\|\,\frac{\bar r\,c}{k+c},\qquad
  o=\mathbf n_{\nabla\phi}\cdot\mathbf n_{\nabla c},
  \label{eq:theory_growth}
\end{equation}
with the unit vectors $\mathbf n_{\nabla\phi}=\nabla_X\phi/\|\nabla_X\phi\|$
and $\mathbf n_{\nabla c}=\nabla_X c/\|\nabla_X c\|$. The factor
$\|\nabla_X\phi\|$ restricts this growth to the interface between biofilm and
void. Consumption is linear in the biofilm as printed (Eq.~24),
$g^*_c=\bar g\,\phi$; the authors confirmed that their implementation uses
$\bar g\,\phi\,c$ (see below).

\subsection{Evolution equations}

Stationarity of~\eqref{eq:hamilton_principle} with respect to $\phi$, $c$ and
$\alpha$ gives three evolution equations. The paper neglects the mechanical
term $\phi\mu(\bI:\mathbf C_e-3)$ in the equation for $\phi$, so that growth
is driven by the nutrient only and the coupling to the mechanics is one-way.
After division by the viscosities, and with the parameters written without the
bar, the equations are, as printed (Eqs.~34--36),
\begin{align}
  &\dot\phi-\beta\nabla_X^2\phi-k_\alpha\,\alpha
   +\|\nabla_X\phi\|\,\frac{r\,c}{k+c}\,\mathbf n_{\nabla\phi}\cdot\mathbf n_{\nabla c}=0,
   \label{eq:theory_phi}\\
  &\dot c-d\,\nabla_X^2 c+g\,\phi=0,
   \label{eq:theory_c}\\
  &\dot\alpha-k_\alpha\,\phi=0,
   \label{eq:theory_alpha}
\end{align}
with zero-flux conditions for $\phi$ and $c$ on the boundary. In the confirmed
form the consumption term in~\eqref{eq:theory_c} is $g\,\phi\,c$. Time is
normalised and written in units of $T^*$. The parameters of Table~2 of the paper are listed in
Table~\ref{tab:theory_klempt2024}.

\begin{table}[htbp]
  \centering
  \caption[Parameters of Klempt et al.\ 2024]{Parameters of Klempt et
    al.~\cite{Klempt2024DiffusionDrivenGrowth}, Table~2. The shear modulus
    corresponds to $E=10$~Pa and $\nu=0.49$.}
  \label{tab:theory_klempt2024}
  \begin{tabular}{llll}
    \hline
    parameter & symbol & value & unit\\
    \hline
    shear modulus & $\mu$ & $3.3557$ & Pa\\

    \hline
  \end{tabular}

Three points of the paper are relevant here.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{The law for $\alpha$.} Equation~\eqref{eq:theory_alpha} lets the
        biofilm expand wherever it is present. The algorithm in Table~1 of the
        paper instead updates $\alpha$ from the change of $\phi$, so that a
        biofilm that is full from the start does not grow. The text says that
        $\alpha$ is governed by Eq.~36; I follow
        Eq.~\eqref{eq:theory_alpha}, as does the element used in
        Chapter~\ref{ch:ansys}.
  \item \emph{The unit of $\mu$.} The text gives $\mu$ in MPa, Table~2 in Pa.
        I use the table.
  \item \emph{The printed growth term.} Solved as printed,
        Eq.~\eqref{eq:theory_phi} moves the seeded colony towards the
        nutrient rather than enlarging it, and the published curves of mean
        $\phi$ are not reached. My reproduction of the paper's test cases
        needs growth on both faces of the colony and a consumption
        proportional to $\phi c$ (Appendix~\ref{app:klempt2024}). The authors
        confirmed the consumption $g\,\phi\,c$; their answers are given in
        the appendix.
\end{itemize}

\section{The Point Model of Klempt et al.\ 2026}
\label{sec:theory_point}

\subsection{State variables}

Klempt et al.~\cite{Klempt2026ContinuumBacterialGrowth} describe several
interacting species at one material point. Species $i=1,\dots,n$ has the volume
fraction $\phi_i\in[0,1]$ and the viability $\psi_i\in[0,1]$, the fraction of
living cells in it. The living volume fraction is
\begin{equation}
  \bar{\phi}_i := \phi_i\,\psi_i, \qquad i = 1,\dots,n.
  \label{eq:living}
\end{equation}
A further fraction $\phi_0$ represents the empty space, and the fractions sum to
one,
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{l=0}^{n} \phi_l(t) = 1 \quad \forall\, t.
  \label{eq:constraint}
\end{equation}
The authors describe $\phi_0$ as a numerical auxiliary variable and caution
against reading it as physical void.

\subsection{Energy, dissipation and evolution equations}

The energy contains the nutrient-driven interaction of the living fractions
and the effect of an antibiotic on the viabilities,
\begin{equation}
  \Psi = -\tfrac{1}{2}\,c^*\,\bar{\bphi}^{\rm T} \bA\,\bar{\bphi}
        + \tfrac{1}{2}\,\alpha^*\,\bpsi^{\rm T} \mathbf{B}\,\bpsi,
  \label{eq:free_energy}
\end{equation}
with the nutrient level $c^*$, the antibiotic level $\alpha^*$, the symmetric
interaction matrix $\bA$ and $\mathbf B=\mathrm{diag}(b_1,\dots,b_n)$. The
constraint~\eqref{eq:constraint} enters through the constraint function
$C=\gamma\bigl(\sum_{l=0}^{n}\phi_l-1\bigr)$ with a Lagrange multiplier $\gamma$,
and the dissipation function is
\begin{equation}
  \Delta = \tfrac{1}{2}\,\dot{\bar{\bphi}}^{\rm T} \boldsymbol{\eta}\,\dot{\bar{\bphi}}
              + \tfrac{1}{2}\,\dot{\bphi}^{\rm T} \boldsymbol{\eta}\,\dot{\bphi},
  \qquad \boldsymbol{\eta} = \mathrm{diag}(\eta_1,\dots,\eta_n).
  \label{eq:dissipation}
\end{equation}
The second term is needed because the first alone gives a singular system:
$\bar\phi_i=\phi_i\psi_i$ couples the two rates.

The stationarity conditions of $\dot\Psi+\Delta+C$ with respect to the rates
$\dot\phi_i$, $\dot\psi_i$, $\dot\phi_0$ and to $\gamma$ give
\begin{align}
  0 &= -c^*\,\psi_i\,\bigl(\bA\bar{\bphi}\bigr)_i
      + \eta_i\bigl(\dot\phi_i\,\psi_i^2 + \bar\phi_i\,\dot\psi_i + \dot\phi_i\bigr)
      + \gamma,
  \label{eq:gov_phi}\\
  0 &= -c^*\,\phi_i\,\bigl(\bA\bar{\bphi}\bigr)_i
      + \alpha^*\,b_i\,\psi_i
      + \eta_i\bigl(\dot\psi_i\,\phi_i^2 + \bar\phi_i\,\dot\phi_i\bigr),
  \label{eq:gov_psi}\\
  0 &= \gamma + \dot\phi_0, \qquad
  0 = \sum_{l=0}^{n}\phi_l - 1 .
  \label{eq:gov_void}
\end{align}
The equation for $\psi_i$ has no $\gamma$, because $\psi_i$ does not appear in
the constraint. The paper prints Eq.~17 with a $+\gamma$; with it, even the
two-species examples of the paper are not reproduced; without it, all fourteen
examples are (Section~\ref{sec:theory_point_check}).

The system~\eqref{eq:gov_phi}--\eqref{eq:gov_void} has $2n+2$ unknowns. It is
integrated with the implicit Euler method and Newton iterations; a logarithmic
barrier with $K_p=\eta_i\times10^{-4}$ keeps $\phi_i$, $\psi_i$ and $\phi_0$
inside $(0,1)$ and is omitted above. Numerical treatments that keep such
constraints satisfied in constitutive models are compared by Bode et
al.~\cite{Bode2025ConstraintConforming}.

\subsection{Symmetry and scale}

The antisymmetric part of $\bA$ does not contribute to the quadratic
form~\eqref{eq:free_energy}, so $\bA$ is taken symmetric; this reduces the
number of interaction coefficients from $n^2$ to $n(n+1)/2$ and keeps the gradient-flow
structure that the variational derivation provides~\cite{JunkerBalzani2021ExtendedHamilton}.
Biologically, symmetry is a reasonable approximation for interactions mediated
by metabolites in a shared pool~\cite{Kolenbrander2010OralMultispecies}; directional
effects such as toxin secretion are excluded.

The nutrient level enters only through the product $c^*\bA$, so $c^*$ and the
scale of $\bA$ cannot be identified separately. In the calibration of
Chapter~\ref{ch:heine} I fix $c^*=25$ and $\eta_i=1$, which keeps the
coefficients of order one; the paper's examples use $c^*=100$ and
species-dependent $\eta_i$. The data of Heine et al.\ involve no antibiotic,
so $\alpha^*=0$ there.

\subsection{Check against the paper}
\label{sec:theory_point_check}

The integrator used in this thesis reproduces all fourteen numerical examples
of the paper (six two-species cases of its Table~1, four variants of its
Appendix~A and the four four-species cases), within the accuracy with which
values can be read off its figures. Two printed details disagree with the
paper's own figures: the $+\gamma$ in Eq.~17 discussed above, and a factor
$\tfrac12$ in front of the four-species matrix of Eq.~19 of Klempt et al.\ 2026;
the four-species figures are reproduced only when it is dropped. For two species, case~3 ends at
$\bar\phi_1=0.5945$, $\bar\phi_2=0.3926$, against $0.60$ and $0.40$ read
from the paper.

\section{Relation to the Replicator Equation}
\label{sec:theory_replicator}

It is useful to relate the point model to the \emph{replicator equation} of
evolutionary game theory~\cite{Hofbauer1998EGT,TaylorJonker1978Replicator},
\begin{equation}
  \dot{\phi}_i = \phi_i \Bigl[ \bigl(\bA\bphi\bigr)_i - \bphi^{\rm T} \bA \bphi \Bigr],
  \qquad \sum_i \phi_i = 1,
  \label{eq:replicator}
\end{equation}
in which the mean fitness $\bphi^{\rm T}\bA\bphi$ keeps the composition on the
simplex. For a symmetric $\bA$, \eqref{eq:replicator} is a gradient flow of
$W(\bphi)=\tfrac12\bphi^{\rm T}\bA\bphi$ with respect to the Shahshahani metric
$g_{ij}=\delta_{ij}/\phi_i$, and $W$ increases monotonically~\cite{Hofbauer1998EGT, Shahshahani1979}.
The point model uses a constant viscosity instead of this $\phi_i$-weighted
metric; with $\psi_i\equiv1$ its well-mixed limit is the affine projection
$\dot\phi_i\propto(\bA\bphi)_i-\langle\bA\bphi\rangle$. The two have the same
fixed points and the same sign structure.

Compared with the generalised Lotka--Volterra model~\eqref{eq:glv}, the
replicator form has a symmetric $\bA$ with $n(n+1)/2$ coefficients and keeps
$\sum_i\phi_i=1$ without a separate normalisation. Its interior fixed points
satisfy $(\bA\bphi^*)_i=\bphi^{*\rm T}\bA\bphi^*$ for every $i$ with
$\phi^*_i>0$. If $\bA$ is negative definite on the tangent space
$\{\mathbf x:\sum_i x_i=0\}$ of the simplex, $W$ is strictly concave there,
so there is at most one interior fixed point; when it exists, it is the
maximum of $W$ and attracts every interior trajectory.

\section{Combining the Two Models}
\label{sec:theory_link}

The field model has a single biofilm variable $\phi$ and says where the
biofilm is and how much there is; it does not distinguish species. The point
model distinguishes species but has no space. Klempt et al.\ 2024 drop the
mechanical term in the equation for $\phi$, so in their model the stress does
not act back on growth. I combine the two models in the same one-way manner:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the field model, solved by the element, gives the amount of biofilm
        $\phi$ at every Gauss point and, through
        Eq.~\eqref{eq:theory_alpha}, the growth $\alpha$ and the stress;
  \item the point model runs at every Gauss point and gives the composition,
        the shares $\phi_i/\sum_j\phi_j$ and the viabilities $\psi_i$, with
        its amount scaled to the field's $\phi$.
\end{itemize}
How the two time scales are linked, and the assumptions this needs, are given
in Chapter~\ref{ch:ansys}. In this one-way form the composition does not act
on the stiffness or on the spreading; a coupling in that direction is outlined
as future work.

\section{Bayesian Inference and TMCMC}
\label{sec:theory_tmcmc}

\subsection{Bayesian parameter identification}

Given observed species trajectories $\mathbf{y} = \{\mathbf{y}_t\}_{t=1}^{N_t}$
and a forward model parameterised by the interaction vector
$\btheta \in \Real^{15}$, Bayesian inference seeks the posterior distribution
\begin{equation}
  p(\btheta \mid \mathbf{y}) \;\propto\; \mathcal{L}(\mathbf{y} \mid \btheta)\,p(\btheta),
  \label{eq:bayes}
\end{equation}
with the likelihood $\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{y} \mid \btheta)$ and the prior
$p(\btheta)$. I use a Gaussian likelihood with isotropic noise,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}(\mathbf{y} \mid \btheta)
  = \prod_{t,i} \mathcal{N}\!\left(y_{t,i} \mid \hat\phi_i(t;\btheta),\,\sigma^2\right),
\end{equation}
where $\hat\phi_i(t;\btheta)$ is the solution of the point model at time $t$.

\subsection{Transitional Markov chain Monte Carlo}

TMCMC~\cite{ChingChen2007TMCMC, Betz2016TMCMC} constructs a sequence of
intermediate distributions between the prior and the posterior through a
tempering schedule $0 = \tau_0 < \tau_1 < \cdots < \tau_J = 1$,
\begin{equation}
  p_j(\btheta) \propto \mathcal{L}(\mathbf{y} \mid \btheta)^{\tau_j}\,p(\btheta).
  \label{eq:tmcmc_intermediate}
\end{equation}
At each stage $j$ the samples are weighted by
$w_j^{(k)} \propto \mathcal{L}^{\tau_j - \tau_{j-1}}$, resampled, and moved
by short Metropolis--Hastings chains. The next exponent $\tau_j$ is chosen so
that the coefficient of variation of the weights stays at a target value,
typically $c_v=1$.

\paragraph{Computation on the GPU.}
The cost lies in the forward model, evaluated for every sample at every stage.
I compile the point model with JAX~\cite{jax2018github} and evaluate the whole
ensemble, $N_p = 10{,}000$ samples in the production runs, in one batched call
(\texttt{jax.vmap}). This is about 150 times faster than the CPU baseline
(Section~\ref{sec:heine_gpu}).

\paragraph{Narrowed priors.}
For the multimodal posterior of the dysbiotic condition I run TMCMC twice: the
mean and standard deviation of the first posterior define a narrower prior for
the second run.

\paragraph{Identifiability.}
To see which parameters the data determine, I use the ratio
\begin{equation}
  r_i \;=\; \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{post},i}}{\Delta_{\mathrm{prior},i}}
  \label{eq:effdim}
\end{equation}
of the posterior standard deviation of $\theta_i$ to its prior width. A value
$r_i \ll 1$ means the data constrain $\theta_i$; $r_i \to 1$ means the prior
does. In Chapter~\ref{ch:heine} all 15 entries have $r_i < 0.43$.

\section{Metabolic Sign Priors}
\label{sec:theory_fba}

Where the data are too sparse to determine the interaction coefficients, their
signs can be fixed beforehand from metabolic models. Flux balance analysis
(FBA) predicts the metabolic fluxes $\mathbf v$ of an organism at steady
state~\cite{OrthThiele2010WhatIsFBA, Heinken2023AGORA2},
\begin{equation}
  \max_{\mathbf{v}}\; \mathbf{w}^{\rm T}\mathbf{v}
  \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \mathbf{N}\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0},\; \mathbf{lb} \le \mathbf{v} \le \mathbf{ub},
\end{equation}
with the stoichiometric matrix $\mathbf N$, an objective $\mathbf w$ (usually biomass
production) and bounds on the fluxes. Parsimonious FBA~\cite{Lewis2012pFBA}
additionally minimises the total flux, and MICOM~\cite{Diener2020MICOM}
couples the models of several species through shared metabolites. From the
predicted exchange between two species follows the sign of their interaction:
a species that feeds another implies $A_{ij}>0$, competition implies
$A_{ij}<0$.

The sign prior $\mathbf S\in\{-1,0,+1\}^{n\times n}$ enters the inference as a
penalty on coefficients of the wrong sign,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{R}(\btheta; \mathbf{S}) = \lambda_S \sum_{i,j:\, S_{ij}\neq 0}
  \max\!\bigl(0,\; -S_{ij}\,A_{ij}\bigr)^2,
\end{equation}
with the weight $\lambda_S$, and leaves coefficients of the right sign free. Only the sign is used, not the
magnitude: predicted interaction strengths of genome-scale models agree poorly
with measured ones, while their signs carry useful
information~\cite{Rath2024GEMReliability}, and no derivation links metabolic
fluxes to the dimensionless coefficients of $\bA$~\cite{Marsland2020MiCRM, Mustri2025GLVABreakdown}.
Section~\ref{sec:heine_signprior} applies this prior to in-vivo data.

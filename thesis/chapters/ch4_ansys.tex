This chapter contains the second and third contributions of the thesis (Section~\ref{sec:intro_structure}):
the growth law $\mathbf F_g=\alpha\mathbf I$ as an ANSYS user material, verified and coupled to the biofilm

\section{The constitutive model}
\label{sec:ch5-model}

% Source: Kinematics: Klempt2024DiffusionDrivenGrowth.
Growth enters multiplicatively,
$\mathbf F = \mathbf F_e \mathbf F_v \mathbf F_g$ with
$\mathbf F_g = \alpha\mathbf I$ and $\alpha(0)=1$, as in Klempt et
al.~\cite{Klempt2024DiffusionDrivenGrowth}; $\alpha-1$ is the growth, and
$\alpha=1$ means no growth. The elastic response is compressible
Mooney--Rivlin, reducing to neo-Hookean at $C_{01}=0$. A viscous branch, a
deviatoric Newtonian dashpot carrying $\mathbf F_v$, is implemented but
switched off ($\eta=0$) in every result of this chapter, as in the paper.

Written out in the order the implementation evaluates it, a step takes the
total deformation gradient $\mathbf F$ and the viscous state
$\mathbf F_v^{\,n}$ and returns the Cauchy stress and $\mathbf F_v^{\,n+1}$:
\begin{enumerate}
\item the mechanical part removes the growth,
      $\mathbf F_{\mathrm{mech}} = \mathbf F\,\mathbf F_g^{-1}$, and a trial
      elastic gradient follows from the viscous state at the start of the step,
      $\mathbf F_e^{\mathrm{tr}} = \mathbf F_{\mathrm{mech}}(\mathbf F_v^{\,n})^{-1}$;
\item with $\mathbf b_e = \mathbf F_e \mathbf F_e^{\mathsf T}$ and
      $J_e = \det \mathbf F_e$, the deviatoric Kirchhoff stress follows from
      $W = C_{10}(\bar I_1 - 3) + C_{01}(\bar I_2 - 3) + \tfrac{1}{D_1}(J_e-1)^2$,
      with the isochoric invariants $\bar I_1$, $\bar I_2$ of $\mathbf b_e$;
\item the viscous state advances by one step of the deviatoric flow rule,
      \begin{equation}
        \mathbf F_v^{\,n+1} =
        \Bigl( \mathbf I + \frac{\Delta t}{2\,\eta\,J_e}\,
        \bm\tau_{\mathrm{dev}} \Bigr)\mathbf F_v^{\,n},
        \label{eq:ch5-flow}
      \end{equation}
      with $\bm\tau_{\mathrm{dev}}$ taken at the trial state;
\item the stress is recomputed at $\mathbf F_v^{\,n+1}$, the volumetric term
      $\tfrac{2}{D_1}(J_e-1)$ is added, and the result is returned in the
      solver's Voigt ordering.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{../ch5_flow/flow_growth_kinematics_standalone.pdf}
  \caption[Multiplicative growth kinematics]{%
    The configurations the decomposition introduces. Growth carries the
    reference state to an intermediate one that is stress free but generally
    incompatible; the elastic part restores compatibility, and this is where
    the residual stress comes from.}
  \label{fig:ch5-kinematics}
\end{figure}

A body that can grow freely therefore stays stress free; stress appears only
where growth is constrained, by boundary conditions or by neighbouring
material that grows less.

% Source: Klempt 2024 Eq. 20 and Table 2; biofilm_material_v01.f, sYoungL < 0.
\paragraph{Stiffness.} Klempt et al.\ weight the elastic energy with
$\phi^2$, so that the void, $\phi=0$, has no stiffness, and give
$\mu=3.3557$~Pa ($E=10$~Pa, $\nu=0.49$) in their Table~2. The routine
supports this form,
\begin{equation}
  E(\phi) = (\phi^2+f)\,E_{\mathrm{bio}},\qquad \nu=\nu_{\mathrm{bio}},
  \label{eq:ch5-stiffness}
\end{equation}
where $f$ is a small floor that keeps the void solvable; it is not in the
paper, whose void has no stiffness. It is tested to
reproduce the plain law fed the same $E$ at every $\phi$ and to scale the
stress by $\phi^2$. The paper's material is incompressible, with $\det\mathbf F_e=1$ enforced by
a Lagrange multiplier, and $\nu=0.49$ enters there only through $\mu$; here
the constraint is approximated by $\nu=0.49$. The element, SOLID185 in its default
$\bar{\mathbf B}$ formulation, integrates over the $2\times2\times2$ Gauss points
of each element, where it also stores its fields, and uses one dilatation per
element, the same idea as the H1P0 element of the paper. A Python solution of the same problem shows
that with this formulation the mean stress changes by less than $10\,\%$
between $\nu=0.49$ and $0.499$, while full integration would overestimate it
several times (\S\ref{sec:ch5-results}).

\section{Implementation}
\label{sec:ch5-impl}

% Source: ansys_usermat/usermat_biofilm.f, biofilm_material_v01.f.
The law is implemented as an ANSYS \texttt{USERMAT}.%
\footnote{\texttt{ansys\_usermat/usermat\_biofilm.f} in the accompanying
repository.}
It is delivered to the partner framework as a routine with the same argument
shape as the framework's own elastic law: per-phase $(E,\nu)$ blended by the
local biofilm fraction, returning Cauchy stress and consistent tangent. The
delivered routine is an adapter around the verified core. This was tested:
fed $(E,\nu)$, it reproduces the core fed the
$(C_{10},C_{01},D_1)$ those map to, at zero tolerance. It uses no solver
includes and no common blocks, so it does not depend on the ANSYS release.

% Source: tests/test_tangent_quality.py -- 648 combinations vs exact AD.
The consistent tangent is obtained by perturbing $\mathbf F$ rather than in
closed form. A tangent that is slightly wrong does not give a wrong answer but
a Newton iteration that converges slowly or not at all, which is hard to
diagnose inside someone else's framework. I therefore compared it with
forward-mode automatic differentiation over the range the routine is expected
to see: stiffness over three decades, Poisson ratios up to $0.49$, growth up to
$\alpha-1=0.35$, both material paths, elastic and viscous. Over 648 combinations
the relative difference has median $1.2\times10^{-7}$ and worst case
$1.1\times10^{-6}$, with no part of the range degrading.

\section{Verification of the growth law}
\label{sec:ch5-verification}

The checks of this section are verification: they test whether the
implementation solves the equations it states. Validation against a measured
stress is not attempted (Section~\ref{sec:ch5-verification-limits}).

\subsection{Growth kinematics against closed forms}
% Source: ansys_usermat/apdl/ free and constrained decks; closed_form_reference.py.
Two growth cases have closed forms. Traction-free isotropic growth expands the
body to $\mathbf F=\alpha\mathbf I$ with zero stress. Fully constrained
growth gives an isotropic elastic deformation, so the deviatoric stress
vanishes and only
\begin{equation}
  \bm\sigma = \frac{2}{D_1}\,(J_e-1)\,\mathbf I,\qquad J_e=\alpha^{-3}
  \label{eq:ch5-constrained}
\end{equation}
remains, independently of the shear constants and of the viscosity.

In ANSYS 2022~R2, one SOLID185 element at $\alpha=1.05$, with
$C_{10}=2\times10^{-4}$~MPa, $C_{01}=0$ and $D_1=5\times10^{3}$~MPa$^{-1}$
(shear and bulk modulus both $4\times10^{-4}$~MPa, $\nu=0.125$; this deck sets
no unit system, so stresses come out in the unit of $C_{10}$, read here as MPa
as in the framework's decks), returns a
largest stress component of $1.9\times10^{-11}$~MPa under free growth, seven
decades below the constrained answer. Under full constraint it returns
$\sigma_{xx}=\sigma_{yy}=\sigma_{zz}=-1.0193\times10^{-4}$~MPa, with shear
components at machine noise. The free case is needed as well: full constraint
can hide a sign error in $\mathbf F_e=\mathbf F\mathbf F_g^{-1}$, because the
element cannot move, and only the free case exposes it.

The constrained value is not equation~\eqref{eq:ch5-constrained} alone. It
equals that term plus a spherical term that I predicted in closed form from
the implementation (\S\ref{sec:ch5-verification-limits}), to every digit
ANSYS prints, with the extra term making up $47\,\%$ of the reported stress
for this material ($\nu=0.125$) (Figure~\ref{fig:ch5-closedform}).

\subsection{The delivered routine on a larger mesh}
% Source: Curved two-layer shell, 12 240 elements, adapter vs core in ANSYS.
A two-layer curved shell of $12\,240$ elements, a non-growing substrate under
a growth layer, was solved twice in ANSYS: once with the verified core and once
with the delivered routine. The von Mises fields agree to every digit reported
(minimum $4.3523\times10^{-9}$, maximum $1.1862\times10^{-5}$, mean
$9.1443\times10^{-6}$, all in MPa), with no solver errors. This is a consistency
check between two builds, not a comparison with a closed form.

The same model showed a meshing error that I corrected. The two layers had been
meshed separately and joined by merging coincident nodes, and on the curved
sweeps only $63$ of $5\,673$ interface nodes coincided, three axial lines. The
growth layer was held along those lines and free between them; the ``two-lobe''
bulge first read as a buckling mode was the layer lifting
between them. With one shared interface mesh the bulge is near uniform and
converges at close to second order: the $\theta$-profile error against the
finest mesh falls from $2.7\,\%$ to $0.58\,\%$ over $2.4$k, $19$k and
$150$k elements, each step halving the element size, an observed order of
$1.9$, and every growth value up
to $\alpha-1=0.2$ converges (Figure~\ref{fig:ch5-alpha-sweep}).

\subsection{What these checks establish, and what they do not}
\label{sec:ch5-verification-limits}
% Source: DEVIATOR_SCALING_FINDING.md.
The isochoric split in the implementation applies $J^{-2/3}$ to the subtracted
trace but not to the tensor beside it. The coded and the correct forms agree
only at $J=1$, and growth moves $J_e$ away from $1$ by
construction. The difference is purely spherical for any deformation, so it is
a pressure error: the deviatoric stress, and with it the von Mises stress
reported in this work, is unaffected at a given deformation. It is the
spherical term of Figure~\ref{fig:ch5-closedform}.

Two of the usual checks cannot see such an error. The free-growth case lands
exactly on $J_e=1$, where both forms agree, and reference values generated by
calling the implementation record its behaviour rather than test it. Only the
comparison with an independently derived closed form finds it, which is why
equation~\eqref{eq:ch5-constrained} is derived from the continuum statement
and not from the code. A wrong pressure can still reach the von Mises stress
indirectly, through the displacement field of an equilibrium solve; this route
is not measured here. It shrinks as $\nu\to0.5$: at the paper's $\nu=0.49$
the extra term is $1.3\,\%$ of the constrained pressure, against $31\,\%$ at
$\nu=0.3$.

\section{Coupling to the element's biofilm field}
\label{sec:ch5-coupling}

\subsection{The framework and the growth equation}
% Source: OLIVER_MODEL_NOTES.md; Klempt 2024 Eq. 34-36.
The partner framework solves the mechanics in ANSYS and its transport fields in
its own routines, alternating substep by substep
(Figure~\ref{fig:partner-loop}). The biofilm field follows Klempt et
al.~\cite{Klempt2024DiffusionDrivenGrowth}, Eq.~34 and 36:
\begin{equation}
  \dot\phi = \beta\,\Delta\phi + k_\alpha\,\alpha
   - r\,\frac{c}{k+c}\,\nabla\phi\cdot\frac{\nabla c}{\|\nabla c\|},
  \qquad \dot\alpha = k_\alpha\,\phi ,
  \label{eq:ch5-field}
\end{equation}
with diffusion, a local source, and a front term \fterm{} that moves the biofilm
boundary up the nutrient gradient. The element computes $\phi$ at its
Gauss points; I leave its equations unchanged. Its Laplacian and the gradients of
$\phi$ and $c$ are approximated from the values at the neighbouring
Gauss points, by a second-order Taylor expansion fitted in the weighted
least-squares sense, the Neighbored Element Method
\cite{Blaszczyk2022TaylorWLS,Rudolf2025NEM}. The field is updated
explicitly, which limits the time step. Rudolf et al.~\cite{Rudolf2025NEM}
give $\Delta t\le h^2/(2\beta)$ for the element size $h$, which is the limit of
the three-point stencil in one dimension; the seven-point stencil in three
dimensions has $\lambda=\beta\Delta t/h^2\le1/6$. A Bloch analysis of the
NEM stencil on the lattice of the Gauss points
(Appendix~\ref{app:stability}) gives the limit of this element: $0.18$ for the
unweighted fit, because the lattice is not uniform, and lower on coarse
meshes, because the width $\beta^*=0.2$~mm of the Gaussian weight is fixed in
millimetres. On $16^3$ and $24^3$ the limit also depends on which points of
the last, equidistant shell enter the fit of $30$ neighbours, and lies
between $0.122$ and $0.140$ on $16^3$ and between $0.122$ and $0.162$ on
$24^3$; on $8^3$ it is $0.062$. Runs to $T^*=5$ confirm this: on $8^3$ they
stay smooth at $\lambda=0.048$ and stop at $0.080$, on $16^3$ at $0.136$ and
$0.144$, and on $24^3$ at $0.130$ and $0.158$ (Figure~\ref{fig:ch5-stability}).
Runs to $T^*=1.1$ are too short to show an instability near the limit. The
results of this chapter use $\lambda\le0.04$ on $8^3$ and $\lambda\le0.072$
on $24^3$, below the band, and $\lambda\le0.128$ on $16^3$, inside the band
but below $0.136$, where the runs to $T^*=5$ on $16^3$ still stay smooth. The
time-step series below has two further cases on $24^3$: at $\Delta t=0.05$
($\lambda=0.144$, inside the band) the seed growth follows the first-order
trend of the other steps, and at $\Delta t=0.1$ ($\lambda=0.288$) the run gave
no result, so the $24^3$ series starts at $\Delta t=0.05$.

What I add sits in the material routine, at every Gauss point: take $\phi$
from the field, integrate the second equation of~\eqref{eq:ch5-field},
\begin{equation}
  \alpha_{n+1}=\alpha_n+k_\alpha\,\phi\,\Delta t ,
  \label{eq:ch5-eq36}
\end{equation}
and pass $\alpha$ to the growth law. I use the paper's $k_\alpha=10^{-3}$ per unit
of normalised time $T^*$, in the field and in~\eqref{eq:ch5-eq36} alike, and
the deck time is $T^*$.

The paper prints a second form of this law (Section~\ref{sec:theory_field}). Table~1, step~3a, of Klempt et
al.\ 2024 integrates $\alpha$ from the change of $\phi$,
$(\alpha_{n+1}-\alpha_n)/((1+\alpha_{n+1})\Delta t)=k_\alpha(\phi_{n+1}-\phi_n)/\Delta t$
(as printed, with $k_\alpha/\eta_\alpha$ read as $k_\alpha$), while its text states that
$\alpha$ follows Eq.~36. The two differ in where growth happens. On the
reproduced field of test case~4.1, at $T^*=1$, biofilm present from the start
reaches $\alpha-1=9.9\times10^{-4}$ with Eq.~36 and stays at zero with
Table~1, and newly colonised biofilm reaches $5.4\times10^{-4}$ and
$8.5\times10^{-4}$ (Figure~\ref{fig:ch5-alpha-law}). A seed that is full
from the start, as in the runs of this chapter, grows only under Eq.~36. I
follow the text, Eq.~36, as does the partner's element.

\subsection{Growth counted once per increment}
% Source: phi_trace checks on IKMHIWI03; 46 calls / 11 substeps.
ANSYS calls the material routine several times per substep, once per
equilibrium iteration and once more for output, and hands back the converged
state each time. Equation~\eqref{eq:ch5-eq36} must therefore start from the
stored state variable and must not accumulate over iterations. A trace of every
call (46 calls over 11 substeps in the first run) confirms that growth is
counted once per increment, that each increment starts from the previous one's
end value, and that $\alpha$ follows~\eqref{eq:ch5-eq36} exactly.

\subsection{Four properties of the framework found along the way}
% Source: ONE_SPECIES_COUPLING.md, stage 3/4 and result-set-1 sections.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{The element's own growth variable is a mean.} The element already
      integrates $\dot\alpha=k_\alpha\phi$ for each of its two species fields
      and uses their mean as the growth of the material. With one species the
      unused field stays at $\alpha=1$, so the growth is halved. Whether the
      mean is intended is a question for the framework's author.
\item \emph{Start values.} The void fraction is $1-\sum\phi_i$, so start
      values must satisfy $\phi_1+\phi_2\le1$. The framework's example
      input starts both species at $1$ in the seed; for one species I start the
      second at $0$.
\item \emph{The first result set is not in equilibrium.} While the element
      sets up its operators it returns zero stress and tangent on its first
      calls, so the solver converges the first substep on a zero residual. The
      output written for that substep is not an equilibrium state and is
      excluded from every result below.
\item \emph{The front term \fterm{} was switched off, and differs from the paper.}
      An input-reading slip sets the orientation weight of species~1 to zero
      (the weights of both species are read into the same variable, and the
      last one read is zero), so the front term of~\eqref{eq:ch5-field} never acts and the biofilm only
      diffuses. The term is also multiplied by $|\Delta\phi|$ where the paper has $\|\nabla\phi\|$. With the
      slip fixed, $|\Delta\phi|$ makes the explicit update unstable, and $\|\nabla\phi\|$ with a central
      gradient lets $\phi$ exceed $1$ (cell P\'eclet number about $30$). Where the nutrient is uniform the
      direction $\nabla c/\|\nabla c\|$ is undefined. The one-species checks of \S\ref{sec:ch5-results} do
      not depend on this term, so the example model there shows no spreading. For the two-species examples
      of Section~\ref{sec:ch5-two-species} the term is on, with the slip fixed, the paper's
      $\|\nabla\phi\|$, the direction regularised as in the authors' implementation (F. Klempt, personal
      communication, October 2026) and a stabilised discretisation.
\end{enumerate}

\section{The point model at every Gauss point}
\label{sec:ch5-composition}

% Source: Klempt 2026; klempt2026_reproduction.py; fritsch2025_cases.py.
\paragraph{The point model.} Klempt et al.~\cite{Klempt2026ContinuumBacterialGrowth}
describe each species $i$ by its volume fraction $\phi_i$ and its living
fraction $\psi_i$, with the void $\phi_0=1-\sum_i\phi_i$:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
(\eta_{\phi,i}+\eta_i\psi_i^2)\,\dot\phi_i+\eta_i\phi_i\psi_i\,\dot\psi_i
&= c^*\psi_i\textstyle\sum_j A_{ij}\phi_j\psi_j-\gamma-(\text{barrier}),\\
\eta_i\,(\phi_i\psi_i\,\dot\phi_i+\phi_i^2\,\dot\psi_i)
&= c^*\phi_i\textstyle\sum_j A_{ij}\phi_j\psi_j-\alpha^* b_i\,\psi_i-(\text{barrier}),
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:ch5-point}
\end{equation}
where $A_{ij}$ are the interactions, $b_i$ the sensitivity to an antibiotic of
concentration $\alpha^*$, $c^*$ the nutrient, $\gamma$ the multiplier for
$\sum_i\phi_i+\phi_0=1$, and the barrier terms keep $\phi_i,\psi_i$
in $(0,1)$; $\eta_i$ and $\eta_{\phi,i}$ are the dissipation parameters of the two
terms of~\eqref{eq:dissipation}, equal to each other in Chapter~\ref{ch:heine}.
My implementation reproduces all fourteen numerical examples of the
paper. Two printed details had to be read differently to do so: Eq.~17 as
printed carries a $+\gamma$ that the figures do not show, and the four-species
interaction matrix of Eq.~19 reproduces the figures without its prefactor
$\tfrac12$. The same model reproduces the figures of Fritsch et
al.~\cite{Fritsch2025BayesianMicrofilms} within $0.013$.

% Rewritten 20-21 Oct 2026 for the coupling agreed with the partner (MEETING_2026-10-20.md,
% thesis/CH4_REWRITE_PLAN.md). Python: coupled_living_consumption.py; ANSYS: usermat mode 11 (A-19).
\paragraph{The point model as published.}
Klempt et al.\ formulate their model on a material point, with $\phi_0$ as an auxiliary variable that
enforces $\sum_i\phi_i+\phi_0=1$ and is not to be read as a physical void
\cite{Klempt2026ContinuumBacterialGrowth}. I therefore do not change the point model: its
equations~\eqref{eq:ch5-point}, its parameters and its constraint stay as published. It is called at
every Gauss point with given inputs and returns its state, which is carried from one increment to the
next as state variables of the Gauss point. The field keeps its own amount $\phi_{3D}$. Throughout,
$\phi_{3D}$ denotes the field of Klempt et al.\ 2024 and $\phi_i$ the point model.

\paragraph{From the field to the point model.}
The element solves the nutrient $c$ of Klempt et al.\ 2024, held at given sources and diffusing through
the domain. As $\phi_{3D}$, the nutrient is a field of the integration points themselves (the
neighboured element method solves it on the Gauss points and the surface points), so the material
routine receives the value at its own Gauss point directly, without an interpolation from nodes. The point model needs $c^*$ at its own, much smaller time steps.
Between the increments $t_n$ and $t_{n+1}$ I interpolate linearly,
\begin{equation}
c^*(t)=c^*_0\Bigl[\tfrac{t_{n+1}-t}{\Delta t}\,c_n+\tfrac{t-t_n}{\Delta t}\,c_{n+1}\Bigr],
\qquad t_n\le t\le t_{n+1},
\label{eq:ch5-cstar}
\end{equation}
with $c_n,c_{n+1}$ the nutrient at the Gauss point and $c^*_0$ the nutrient level of the point model's
own examples. Klempt et al.\ 2026 already use a time-dependent $c^*$ (their four-species case~3), so
the point model accepts such an input without change. The field of Klempt et al.\ 2024 has no
antibiotic. The element solves a second nutrient for its second species, which I do not use; I use this
field for an antibiotic $a$, with its own sources and the same diffusion, and pass
$\alpha^*(t)=\alpha^*_0\,a$ to the point model, interpolated as in~\eqref{eq:ch5-cstar}. Because the
element advances its fields after the material routine, the pair $(c_n,c_{n+1})$ is the one of the
previous increment. A short first load step, one internal step of the element's field solver
($0.0025$ in $T^*$), solves the fields before the point model starts, so the point model begins with the
solved nutrient (in the seed $c=0.79$) and the living biomass of its start state already enters the
first consumption.

\paragraph{From the point model to the field.}
Only living biomass consumes nutrient. The consumption of Eq.~35 of Klempt et al.\ 2024 is printed as
$g\phi$; it is $g\phi_{3D}c$, and the printed form is a misprint.
In general the uptake depends on the nutrient through a Monod-type factor $f(c)=c/(K_c+c)$; for
$K_c\gg c$ this is $c/K_c$, and with $1/K_c$ absorbed in $g$ it is the linear form $g\phi_{3D}c$ used here.
The time derivative of Eq.~35 is kept. With the diffusivity of Table~2 of Klempt et al.\ 2024, $d=10^{10}~\mu$m$^2/T^*$
on their $20~\mu$m cube, converted like $\beta$ to $d=10^8$~mm$^2/T^*$ on the $2$~mm cube, the nutrient adjusts within
about $4\cdot10^{-8}\,T^*$, so the fields are solved quasi-steadily; with the time derivative and this $d$ the seed values
change by less than $0.005$ (Python). The consumption follows the same table: $g=10^8\,T^{*-1}$, so that $g\ell^2/d=1$ with the half edge
$\ell=1$~mm. The antibiotic, carried by the element's second nutrient field, has the same diffusivity and uptake as the nutrient. Only a diffusion
slower by about eight orders of magnitude would matter: with $d=1$~mm$^2/T^*$, the value of the element's example
input, the antibiotic reaches the seed more slowly and kills markedly less of it.
I scale it by the living volume
fraction of the point model,
\begin{equation}
\dot c=d\,\nabla^2c-g\,\phi_{3D}\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^N\bar\phi_i\Bigr)c,\qquad \bar\phi_i=\phi_i\psi_i,
\label{eq:ch5-consumption}
\end{equation}
in the notation of Klempt et al.\ 2026. The point model normalises its volume, so $\bar\phi_i\in[0,1]$
is the living volume fraction of species $i$ at the point, and $\phi_{3D}\sum_i\bar\phi_i\le\phi_{3D}$
is the living volume fraction in the field. A dead biofilm consumes nothing; the void $\phi_0$ has no
living fraction and is not part of the sum. The antibiotic is taken up in the same way,
$\dot a=d_a\nabla^2a-g_a\phi_{3D}\bigl(\sum_i\bar\phi_i\bigr)a$. The growth law~\eqref{eq:ch5-eq36} is
unchanged: the inflation $\alpha$ measures how much biomass has been produced, alive or dead. The
spreading of $\phi_{3D}$ follows Eq.~34 of Klempt et al.\ 2024 with its front term \fterm{}, which depends on
$c$; this is the path by which the point model reaches the growth and the stress. An antibiotic acts on
the stress only through it: it lowers $\psi_i$, so less nutrient is consumed and more reaches the front.

\paragraph{Time.}
Klempt et al.\ 2024 normalise time with a reference time chosen for the problem at hand. I choose
$T^*=1$ as the duration covered by the point model's data, so that both models run on the same time: the
point model advances $s\,\Delta T^*$ per increment, with $s$ the length of that window in its own units,
not a fitted value. For the synthetic data of Fritsch et al.~\cite{Fritsch2025BayesianMicrofilms},
case~I (1000 steps of $10^{-4}$), $s=0.1$. With synthetic four-species data and this choice, a point
model that keeps its own constraint reproduces the point model alone to $7\times10^{-7}$; scaled to the
field amount instead, the error is $0.019$, and a value of $s$ fitted to the data comes out a third too
low because it absorbs that error.

\paragraph{Start values.}
The point model starts at a Gauss point once $\phi_{3D}$ there reaches $\phi_{\min}$. Where a seed is
placed, the start values are those of the seed (one species, or a mixture). A point that the spreading
biofilm reaches later takes the living composition of its colonised neighbours: their amounts are
divided in the ratio of their living volume fractions $\bar\phi_i=\phi_i\psi_i$, and the new point starts
alive ($\psi_i=0.999$, $\gamma=0$). I chose this rule because the biofilm spreads through the growth of
its living cells. The papers do not fix it; the alternative of starting from the seed composition at the
nearer seed is compared in Section~\ref{sec:ch5-results}. The point model has no transport between
points, so the composition moves with the biofilm only through this rule.

\paragraph{Assumptions.} The coupling rests on choices that neither paper fixes, each with its
sensitivity in Section~\ref{sec:ch5-results}: the linear interpolation of $c^*$ and $\alpha^*$ in time;
$\sum_i\bar\phi_i$ taken from the previous increment; the start values of newly reached points; the
threshold $\phi_{\min}$; and, for the examples, the uptake of the antibiotic. The time link follows from
the data (above).

\paragraph{The complete step.} Algorithm~\ref{alg:ch5-coupled} collects one increment of the coupled
run. ANSYS solves the equilibrium $\operatorname{div}\boldsymbol\sigma=\mathbf 0$ by Newton's method;
the material routine turns the deformation at a Gauss point into a stress after removing the growth and
advances the point model. Its state does not enter the stress, so it does not affect the Newton
iteration.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{One increment $t_n\to t_{n+1}$ of the coupled run}
\label{alg:ch5-coupled}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE at every Gauss point $\alpha_n$, the point-model state $(\phi_i,\psi_i,\gamma)_n$ and the pair
  $(c,a)$ seen in the previous increment; the element's fields $\phi_{3D}$, $c$, $a$ of $t_n$
\ENSURE displacements, stresses and state of $t_{n+1}$
\REPEAT[Newton iteration of ANSYS]
  \FORALL{Gauss points}
    \STATE $\alpha_{n+1}\leftarrow\alpha_n+k_\alpha\,\phi_{3D}\,\Delta t$ \COMMENT{Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch5-eq36}}
    \STATE $\mathbf F_e\leftarrow\mathbf F\,\mathbf F_g^{-1}$, $\mathbf F_g=\alpha_{n+1}\mathbf I$; stress and tangent from $W(\mathbf F_e)$ and $E(\phi_{3D})$
    \IF{$\phi_{3D}\ge\phi_{\min}$ or the point model has started}
      \IF{not yet advanced in this increment}
        \STATE $(c_n,c_{n+1})$, $(a_n,a_{n+1})$ from the previous and the current value at the Gauss point
        \STATE $n_{\rm sub}$ steps of $s\Delta t/n_{\rm sub}$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch5-point} with $c^*$, $\alpha^*$ from~\eqref{eq:ch5-cstar}
        \STATE store $(\phi_i,\psi_i,\gamma)_{n+1}$ and $\sum_i\bar\phi_i$
      \ENDIF
    \ENDIF
  \ENDFOR
\UNTIL{the residual meets the convergence criterion}
\STATE the element advances $\phi_{3D}$ (Eq.~34 with the front term \fterm{}), $c$ and $a$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch5-consumption} with $\sum_i\bar\phi_i$)
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

converted to the 2~mm cube. The conversion is an assumption: the paper gives
no length and time scale, and its first author describes $\beta$ as chosen
for sensible results and dependent on the mesh. The framework's example
input, $\beta=10^{-4}$~mm$^2/T^*$, is reported as a sensitivity case. The
remaining coefficients of the field and of the nutrient are those of the
framework's example input (\texttt{/UNITS,MPA}, lengths in mm), not values from the paper: $r=100$~mm$/T^*$,

examples of Section~\ref{sec:ch5-two-species} differ: they use the constants of the

\paragraph{Exact solution inside ANSYS.} Where the field has no gradients,
\eqref{eq:ch5-field} reduces to $\dot\phi=k_\alpha\alpha$,

$\alpha=\cosh(k_\alpha t)$. ANSYS follows the exact solution of its own time
stepping to $3\times10^{-11}$ in $\alpha$ (round-off) and $1.1\times10^{-7}$
in $\phi$, about $10^{-4}$ relative, because the element still sees a small
gradient from its neighbours (Figure~\ref{fig:ch5-exact}).

\paragraph{Stresses with the published stiffness.} With the stiffness of
Klempt et al.\ 2024, equation~\eqref{eq:ch5-stiffness} with $E=10$~Pa,
$\nu=0.49$ and a void floor $f=10^{-3}$, $\alpha-1$ in a monitored element inside the
seed reaches $8.9\times10^{-4}$ at $T^*=1.1$ on $16^3$ elements
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch5-element}). This is less than $k_\alpha\phi t=1.1\times10^{-3}$,
because $\phi$ diffuses out of the seed. Klempt et
al.~\cite{Klempt2024DiffusionDrivenGrowth,Klempt2023NumericalComparison}
report pressure inside the biofilm and a ring of tension at its edge. Grouped
by distance from the seed, the mean stress is $-2.5\times10^{-4}$~Pa in the
seed, $+6.0\times10^{-5}$~Pa in the first layer of elements around it and
$+1.3\times10^{-6}$~Pa in the second; every element of these two layers is in
tension. The von Mises stress of this element is $4.7\times10^{-4}$~Pa, the seed
average $5.9\times10^{-4}$~Pa (Table~\ref{tab:ch5-mesh}) and the largest in
the model, also inside the seed, $7.2\times10^{-4}$~Pa. The element's own growth
variable gives $0.49$ times the growth and the stress of
equation~\eqref{eq:ch5-eq36}, which is $\tfrac12\times1.1/1.125$ for this time
step (see the whole-model check above). With the framework's example
stiffness ($E=1000$~MPa, $\nu=0.3$, blended linearly) the mean stress in the
seed is about $4\times10^{8}$ times larger and the von Mises stress about
$2\times10^{8}$ times. Changing $\nu$ from $0.49$ to $0.45$ or $0.499$
changes the mean stress in the seed by less than $2\,\%$.

The first author of the paper has since suggested that its stiffness unit may
be wrong and that $E=10$~kPa is more realistic. Growth is the only load, so
all stresses in this chapter then scale by $10^3$; the comparisons and ratios
do not change.

\paragraph{Order of convergence.} With the surface points corrected
(\S\ref{sec:ch5-limitations}), I repeated the seed growth on the three meshes
with $\Delta t=0.1$, $0.05$, $0.025$ and $0.0125$, on $24^3$ from $\Delta t=0.05$
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch5-wp3-conv});
the seed values agree with Table~\ref{tab:ch5-mesh} within $0.03\,\%$.
Halving the time step halves the change, so the update is first order in time,
and the error at $\Delta t=0.025$ is about $1\,\%$ in the seed von Mises stress.
A fit through the three meshes alone would give an observed order of about
$2.1$ for the seed von Mises stress; three meshes cannot check this, and a
finer reference shows otherwise. I solved the mechanics with a
separate hexahedral code (mean-dilatation integration, the stiffness of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch5-stiffness}) for the reference growth field described
next, on meshes up to $48^3$, once with one value of $\phi$ and $\alpha$ per
element and once with the values at the $2\times2\times2$ integration
points, as the element carries them. The first series approaches the seed
von Mises stress from above, the second from below; on $48^3$ they give
$6.11$ and $6.07\times10^{-4}$~Pa, so the converged value lies between the
two, $2\,\%$ above the element's value on $24^3$ after extrapolation to
$\Delta t\to0$. With the Gauss-point values the code reproduces the
element's seed stress within $3.5\,\%$ on $8^3$ and within $1\,\%$ on $16^3$
and $24^3$; on the coarse mesh the Gauss points smooth the step in
$\alpha$ at the surface of the seed inside an element, which lowers the
stress. The stress converges only to about first order, because the
stiffness jumps at the surface of the seed.

For the growth field itself the error can be measured. Without the front
term the seed problem is the linear system of Eqs.~\eqref{eq:theory_phi}
and \eqref{eq:theory_alpha} with the penalty of the element, and I solved it
with second-order finite volumes and a fourth-order Runge--Kutta scheme on
$48^3$, $96^3$ and $192^3$ cells (the seed is a union of cells on each grid).
The seed mean of $\alpha-1$ converges with an observed order of $1.97$, and the
Richardson extrapolation of the two finest grids is the reference, with a
remaining error below $0.05\,\%$. Averaged over the elements of each NEM mesh
and compared element by element (Figure~\ref{fig:ch5-wp3-conv}, right), the
element's $\alpha-1$ after extrapolation to $\Delta t\to0$ is too large by
$4.1\,\%$ in the seed mean on $8^3$, $1.3\,\%$ on $16^3$ and $0.7\,\%$ on
$24^3$, an order of about $1.7$ in $h$; the $L_2$ norm of the element-wise
error over the cube is $5.1$, $1.9$ and $1.1\,\%$. The error is largest near
the edge of the seed, where the gradient of $\phi$ is steepest ($5.5\,\%$ at
$0.4$~mm from the centre on $8^3$, $0.2\,\%$ on $24^3$), and smallest at the
centre ($0.5$ to $0.2\,\%$). The domain integral of $\alpha-1$ is within
$0.3\,\%$ on every mesh: the explicit update conserves the mass of $\phi$ up to
the source, and the mesh error redistributes the growth between the seed and
its surroundings. At $\Delta t=0.0125$ the errors are about $0.2\,\%$ larger
than after extrapolation.

\subsection{Two species with nutrient and antibiotic}
\label{sec:ch5-two-species}

\paragraph{Set-up.} The examples use the coupling of Section~\ref{sec:ch5-composition} with the
synthetic data of Fritsch et al.~\cite{Fritsch2025BayesianMicrofilms}, case~I, two species (A and B,
$b=(1,2)$). All constants are those of the papers: the field and the nutrient from Klempt et al.\ 2024,
Table~2, converted to the 2~mm cube ($\beta=0.02$~mm$^2/T^*$, $r=10$~mm$/T^*$, $d=10^8$~mm$^2/T^*$,
$g\ell^2/d=1$, $E=10$~Pa, $\nu=0.49$, void floor $f=10^{-3}$), and the antibiotic of case~4 of Klempt et
al.\ 2026 ($\alpha^*_0=100$ from $T^*=1/3$), with the diffusion and uptake of the nutrient. The
threshold is $\phi_{\min}=0.001$; it is not given by the papers and changes the results by less than
$1\,\%$ below this value. In the separate example species~A fills the seed of this chapter and
species~B four small blocks along the edges of the cube; in the mixed example both start in the seed,
in equal amounts ($\phi_1=\phi_2=0.275$) or in the proportions of Fritsch et al.\ ($0.25$, $0.30$). The
nutrient is held on the face $y=-1$~mm, the antibiotic on all outer faces. The front term \fterm{} of Eq.~34 is
on in every run.

These runs use a finite-volume code in Python that solves the same equations as the element, with the
point model of the ANSYS runs; with uniform fields the coupled point model agrees with the point model
alone to $2\times10^{-14}$. The front term \fterm{} is advection dominated: its cell P\'eclet
number $r h/(2\beta)$ is about $31$ on the $16^3$ mesh, and a central gradient oscillates. I discretise
it with a second-order upwind scheme (MUSCL with the minmod limiter) and mirror the fields at the walls.
The stress is solved on a $16^3$ hexahedral mesh with the element's integration; solving the same
$64^3$ field on $32^3$ and $64^3$ elements changes the seed von Mises stress by $0.2\,\%$.

\paragraph{Time step.} The point model advances once per outer step of the field. The composition
and $\alpha-1$ change by less than $3$ and $0.3\,\%$ between outer steps of $0.02$ and $0.0025$ (with an
integer number of inner steps). The stress does not: in the separate example the step $0.025$ of
Table~\ref{tab:ch5-two-species} gives about $6\,\%$ less than the limit, which first-order
extrapolation puts at about $6.07\times10^{-4}$~Pa on $64^3$; in the mixed example it gives about
$15\,\%$ more ($3.23$ against $2.82\times10^{-4}$~Pa at $0.0025$). The inner steps of the point model,
the one-increment lag of $\sum_i\bar\phi_i$ and the regularisation of the front direction
($\varepsilon^2=10^{-6}$ against $10^{-8}$, $0.1\,\%$) are not the cause. Without antibiotic the step
changes the stress by $2.5\,\%$ (separate) and $12\,\%$ (mixed). I therefore take the growth and the
stress from the grid series at the small step.

\paragraph{Antibiotic over time.} With the same total dose given constantly, in pulses or as a sine
from $T^*=1/3$, $\alpha-1$ and the stress change by less than $1.5\,\%$, while the living volume
fraction follows the dose: in the pauses of the pulsed dose it recovers from $0.16$ to $0.28$ in the
separate example. The antibiotic acts on the composition and the living biomass, not on the mechanics.

\paragraph{Start values of newly reached points.} With the start values of the nearer seed instead of
the neighbours' living composition, the seed values of the cube examples differ by a few percent (living
volume fraction $0.147$ against $0.152$ in the separate example with antibiotic). In the maze of Klempt et
al.\ 2024, Fig.~11, where the biofilm keeps spreading after the antibiotic, the rule decides whether it
recovers: at $T^*=60$ the living volume fraction is $0.35$ with the neighbours' composition and $0.04$ with
the start state.

\paragraph{The element.} The ANSYS element solves the same equations with its own discretisation
(Section~\ref{sec:ch5-coupling}). For these runs I corrected three numerical details of it, without
changing its equations: the surface points take the value of the nearest Gauss point instead of a
distance-weighted mean, the Laplacian is used in a conservative form, and the neighbours of the fit are
chosen by distance instead of by element number. Before, $\phi$ leaked through the walls of the cube. Its growth agrees with the Python values within $4\,\%$ after
extrapolation. Its stress depends on how the front term \fterm{} is stabilised: with a constant added diffusion
$r h/4$ the seed stress on $16^3$ is about a quarter of the converged value, and with upwind gradients it
is $2.14$, $4.56$ and $5.15\times10^{-4}$~Pa on $8^3$, $16^3$ and $24^3$, extrapolated
$5.8\times10^{-4}$~Pa (GCI $16.5\,\%$). This lies within the band of the second-order schemes in Python. The
element runs the point model in every increment of $0.00125$, so its outer-step error is small. I take
the values of this section from the Python code, which reaches finer grids.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:ch5-limitations}

The limitations of the whole work (validation, feedback of the stress, transport of the composition, the
front speed and the assumptions) are collected in Section~\ref{sec:concl_limitations}. The following concern
the implementation of this chapter.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Faces of the cube.} In the element used here, $\phi$ at the surface
      points was not updated and stayed $0$, so every face acted as $\phi=0$
      where Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch5-field} has no flux. With these points set to the
      mean of their neighbouring Gauss points, the runs with
      $\beta=0.02$~mm$^2/T^*$ change by less than $0.13\,\%$ in the seed stress
      and in $\alpha$ and by less than $0.5\,\%$ in the composition, so their
      values are kept. Where $\phi$ reaches the faces (larger $\beta$, longer
      times) the seed pressure changes by up to $30\,\%$; the $8^3$ values of
      Table~\ref{tab:ch5-beta} for $\beta=0.05$ and $0.1$ are those of the
      corrected runs. The mean is exact only for a constant field; in the seed runs the resulting error
      stays in the layer of elements at the faces, where $\alpha-1$ is about $1\,\%$ of its maximum.
\item \emph{Pressure term.} The spherical term of
      \S\ref{sec:ch5-verification-limits} is documented, not corrected. It does
      not change the von Mises stress at a given deformation, and at
      $\nu=0.49$ it is $1.3\,\%$ of the constrained pressure.
\item \emph{Incompressibility.} The paper's exact constraint is approximated by
      $\nu=0.49$ with the element's mean-dilatation formulation; the mean
      stress in the seed changes by less than $2\,\%$ between $\nu=0.45$,
      $0.49$ and $0.499$.
\item \emph{Mesh.} With $\beta=0.02$~mm$^2/T^*$ the mean stress in the seed
      and in the first layer around it agrees within about $3\,\%$ on $8^3$,
      $16^3$ and $24^3$ elements, and the seed von Mises stress within
      $3\,\%$ between $16^3$ and $24^3$; with the example input
      $\beta=10^{-4}$~mm$^2/T^*$ they do not converge. Peak stresses at the seed corners
      are mesh dependent and not reported.
\item \emph{Consumption.} The one-species ANSYS results of this chapter use the consumption
      $g\,\phi$ as printed (Eq.~35); the two-species examples use $g\,\phi_{3D}(\sum_i\bar\phi_i)\,c$, the
      form $g\,\phi\,c$ confirmed by the authors (Appendix~\ref{app:klempt2024}).
\item \emph{Front direction.} The direction $\nabla c/\|\nabla c\|$ of the front term \fterm{} is undefined
      where $\nabla c=\mathbf 0$; I regularise it with
      $\|\nabla c\|^2+\varepsilon^2$, $\varepsilon^2=10^{-6}$, as the authors' implementation does with a
      small $\varepsilon$ (F. Klempt, personal communication, October 2026); $\varepsilon^2=10^{-8}$
      changes the stress by $0.1\,\%$.
\item \emph{Magnitude.} With the published growth rate, $\alpha-1$ stays below
      about $10^{-3}$ up to $T^*=1$, and with the published stiffness the
      largest von Mises stress is about $7\times10^{-4}$~Pa ($0.7$~Pa with
      $E=10$~kPa, the value suggested by the paper's first author).
\end{itemize}

\section{Earlier one-way computations}
\label{sec:ch5-outlook}
\label{sec:ch5-outlook-python}

Before the coupling of Section~\ref{sec:ch5-composition} was agreed, I computed four steps towards a
fuller coupling, mostly in Python, to see which of them change the results: the local nutrient in the point
model, the composition in the spreading and in the stiffness, and the stress in the growth. The first is now
part of the coupling; the others are listed in the outlook (Section~\ref{sec:concl_outlook}). These
computations use the earlier one-way
scheme, in which the composition is scaled to the amount of the field, and cases~3 and~6 of Klempt et
al.\ 2026. The papers give no growth rates or moduli per species; the rates are estimated from the
dissipation parameters $\eta_i$ under a stated assumption, and the moduli are sensitivity parameters.

\paragraph{Step 1: local nutrient.} The nutrient is held at the face $y=-1$~mm and consumed in the seed, and
each point of the seed runs the point model with $c^*=c^*_0\,c$. Case~3 hardly depends on the nutrient level
(share $\phi_1/(\phi_1+\phi_2)$ $0.63$ to $0.66$). In case~6 the takeover is slower where the nutrient is low,
and the share ranges from $0.03$ near the nutrient face to $0.44$ inside the seed (Thiele number
$\Lambda=\sqrt{g\ell^2/d}=4$~\cite{Thiele1939}, $\ell=1$~mm). The same step in ANSYS ($8^3$, case~6,
consumption $6\,T^{*-1}$, chosen in this work) gives a share of $0.11$ to $0.48$, correlated with the local
nutrient ($-0.99$), and Python gives the same range. Over all runs the share at the end depends on the time
link $s$, the end time and the local nutrient only through the product $s\,T^*c$
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch5-clock}): the nutrient acts as the clock of the point model. In case~6 the takeover
happens between $s\,T^*c\approx0.03$ and $0.08$, so the assumed $s$ decides whether a composition pattern is
seen at $T^*=1$.

\paragraph{Step 2: composition into the spreading.} On the reproduced field of
Klempt et al.\ 2024, test case~4.1, the front growth rate becomes
the mean of the species rates weighted by their shares,
$\bar r=\bigl(\phi_1r_1+\phi_2r_2\bigr)/(\phi_1+\phi_2)$, with
$r_{1,2}=(1\pm\delta)\,r$ and $r$ from Table~\ref{tab:theory_klempt2024}; for $\delta=0$ the scheme is the one-way one. The
weighting follows Feng et al.~\cite{Feng2021SymbioticBiofilm}, whose spreading velocity has the summed
species growth as its divergence, and the summed growth sources of Soleimani et
al.~\cite{Soleimani2023CoAggregation}. The rate contrast has two estimates. In cases~3 and~6 of Klempt et
al.\ 2026 the second species has twice the dissipation of the first,
$\eta_1=1$, $\eta_2=2$; assuming that the front rate of a species scales with
$1/\eta_i$ gives $r_1/r_2=2$ and $\delta=1/3$. Feng et al.\ use maximum growth rates
of $3\times10^{-5}$ and $8\times10^{-5}$~s$^{-1}$ for their two species, the
first measured and the second estimated, a ratio of $2.7$ and $\delta\approx0.45$. With $\delta=1/3$
the mean $\phi$ at $T^*=1$ rises from $0.704$ to $0.735$ in case~3, where the
faster species is the majority, and falls to $0.648$ in case~6, where the slower
species takes over; locally $\phi$ changes by up to $0.47$
(Figure~\ref{fig:ch5-two-way}). With $\delta\approx0.45$ from the rates of Feng et al.\ the
two values are $0.746$ and $0.626$, with $\delta=0.5$ they are $0.750$ and $0.618$. The composition itself hardly changes. Which
species wins then decides how fast the biofilm spreads. The values are
converged in the coupling step. On a grid of half the spacing ($41^3$ nodes,
$0.5~\mu$m) the one-way mean $\phi$ falls from $0.704$ to $0.674$, and the
changes for $\delta=0.5$, at a coupling step of $0.05$, shrink from $+6\,\%$ and $-13\,\%$ to $+5\,\%$ and
$-8\,\%$: the sign and the order of the effect hold, its size depends on the
grid of the reproduced field, for which I keep the paper's $1~\mu$m. In ANSYS
this step needs a working front term \fterm{}.

\paragraph{Step 3: composition into the stiffness.} With the seed composition
of step~1 and a ratio of the species moduli up to $5$ at the same mean modulus,
the average stresses in the seed change by at most $0.2\,\%$. The seed is about
a thousand times stiffer than the void around it, so the soft surroundings set
its stress. This step matters only where the biofilm itself carries the load,
such as a contiguous biofilm on a substrate.

\paragraph{Step 4: stress into growth.} In the example model the mean stress
in the seed, about $2.5\times10^{-4}$~Pa, is below $10^{-4}$ times the shear
modulus, so a stress-dependent
growth law would change little with the published parameters, while the
mechanical term of Klempt et al.\ 2024, Eq.~30, kept as written would stop all
growth (Appendix~\ref{app:klempt2024}). This step needs a different growth law
and realistic stress levels.

Of these steps, step~2 changes the results most; step~1 is now part of the coupling.

1         I N T RO DU CT ION

Material and structural failure can be modeled in various ways. For instance, fracture mechanics allows for a detailed
analysis of the stress field at a crack tip.1-3 The eigenerosion method, see References 4 and 5, might be interpreted as a
(numerical) extension of fracture mechanics. Phase-field models are also feasible for the description of failure.6-9 Finally,
damage models might be used since they use the simplest possible assumption that, after reaching some critical threshold
value, stiffness decreases with increasing loads. This might be formulated in terms of isotropic, cf. References 10 and 11,
and anisotropic damage, cf. References 12 and 13. Due to the simple assumptions for damage models, they have been
used for modeling failure events at various scales, see, eg, References 11,14-17, including structural effects such as the
snapback phenomenon.16,18,19
    However, damage models inherently constitute an ill-posed mathematical problem: due to their softening character,
a loss of ellipticity of the resultant system of algebraic equations yields a strong mesh dependence when evaluated within
a finite element routine, see, eg, References 19 and 20. Consequently, methods for regularization have to be employed, of
which three different classes exist: (i) viscous regularization, (ii) integral formulations, and (iii) gradient enhancement.
    Viscous regularization, such as in References 21-26, is the most direct approach for solving the issue of
mesh-dependent finite element results. The main advantage of this strategy is the easy mathematical formulation, which
allows for a numerical implementation that can be carried out at the material point level. To be more precise, the usage of a
material law subroutine is sufficient. Unfortunately, there exists a remarkable drawback that prevents viscous regulariza-
tion to become the dominant strategy chosen: the results explicitly depend on the loading velocity, which might contradict
experimental observations, particularly when brittle material failure is investigated. Furthermore, model calibration is

cumbersome since usually viscosities must be used to obtain mesh independent results that are unphysically high, such
that they do not constitute as a material parameter that is independent of the respective boundary value problem. In con-
trast, loading velocity in combination with the damage viscosity has to be calibrated for each boundary value problem on
an individual basis.
    Integral formulations and gradient enhancement, which show some mathematical similarities to phase field
approaches, share the characteristic that they usually demand a more sophisticated strategy for numerical implementa-
tion: novel finite elements have to be programmed in which the damage evolution is averaged over same neighborhood
of elements (for integral formulations), or in which additional nodal unknowns enter the problem (for gradient enhance-
ment). While these strategies consume more computational effort than viscous regularization, the huge benefit is that
such models yield mesh-independent finite element results for arbitrary loading velocities and can also be used for
modeling brittle damage. Examples are given in References 3, 19, 20, 27, and 28.
    Additional model parameters, such as the damage viscosity for viscous regularization, are also present both for integral
formulations and gradient enhancement which play here a less significant role due to physical reasons but are a mathe-
matical necessity. For integral formulations, the radius of the circle (sphere) over which the averaging is performed has
to be defined. Prominent examples are the so-called filter techniques commonly used for topology optimization problems
(which might be interpreted as “inverse damage” problems).29,30 The regularization parameter used in strategies based on
gradient enhancement has a similar effect. Here, the parameter directly controls the width of the damage zone and also
the transition from the undamaged to the damaged area (large values yield thick damage zones and a very smooth transi-
tion, whereas small values yield crack-like damaged zones with very sharp interfaces between damaged and undamaged
material). A physical interpretation of the regularization parameter is that it reflects the interface energy and that it is
related to the “internal length” given by the crack width, for example, Reference 27.
    Although gradient enhancement yields mesh-independent results, the impact of the chosen penalization parameter
is huge: not only the shape of the damage zone is controlled but also the maximum load before material failure strongly
depends on the particular value for the parameter. In order to resolve a distinct damage zone in greater detail, strategies for
mesh adaptivity can be employed.31 However, in contrast to the accepted mesh dependence in Reference 31 due to missing
regularization, which was assumed to be adjustable to experiments, a rigorous consideration of gradient enhancement
should be maintained to still deal with a well-posed mathematical problem.
    The motivation for this article is to investigate the capabilities of the gradient-enhanced material model for brittle
damage in Reference 32, where the neighbored element method (NEM) was used. It showed the huge advantage that
each global load step could be computed with very similar numerical effort, that is, computation time, as a load step
for a purely elastic material behavior. In this article, we improve the NEM such that is in particular capable of handling
adaptive finite element meshes and present a detailed and advanced study on the numerical behavior of this method for
adaptive treatment of the meshing and load stepping at runtime.
    The article is structured as follows: we begin with a short presentation of the model in Reference 32 for convenience.
Afterward, we present the novel numerical treatment and its implementation, with special emphasis on both the finite
element implementation of the model itself and the adaptive strategies applied. Then, we perform a detailed numerical
study that investigates all important effects concerning convergence behavior and the dependence on the material and
model parameters.

well. This way, the parameter 𝛽 leads to a spreading of the damage or, equivalently, prevents steep gradients in the damage
function.


3.1.1        Elasticity solver

Given the current damage function f û , the displacement of the specimen for an increased loading given in terms
of the prescribed displacements û + dû has to be computed by solving Equation (10). To this end, we employ a
geometric multigrid solver35,36 on a mesh hierarchy produced by successive refinement from an initial coarse finite
element mesh. For an adaptive mesh hierarchy, we will use the adaptive multigrid approach37,38 without smoothing
of the patch rims. In all simulations, we use a Gauss-Seidel smoother, V-cycle, three presmoothing and postsmooth-
ing steps, the canonical prolongation and restriction, the RAP product for coarse matrices, and an LU base solver
on the coarsest mesh. The multigrid is wrapped into a BiCGStab solver as preconditioner, and we solve for a
residuum reduction or an absolute residuum of 10−10 (whatever applies first) with a maximal admissible iteration
count of 200.

3.2           Adaptivity

A crack-like evolution of a damage zone is highly localized both in space and in time, and it is thus advis-
able to address this behavior with adaptive numerical strategies for the computational mesh and for the load
stepping.

3.2.2         Mesh size control

For the mesh size control, we employ adaptive mesh refinement before every load increment computation. Starting with
an initial coarse mesh, we allow for the recursive refinement of mesh elements to increase the mesh resolution locally.
The number of refinements that were applied to create a finite element is called the level of refinement of the element, and
we allow refinements up to an a priori prescribed level Lmax ∈ N. For full refinement, all elements of the finite element
mesh are refined. For adaptive refinement, however, only a selected subset of elements is chosen for further refinement,
and some element patches may also be coarsened if admissible.
     As an indicator for the required mesh resolution, we employ the elementwise value Ψe ∶= fe Ψ0,e . Given that the elastic
energy, Ψ0,e , is large, the element is likely to damage in the future and should be refined up to a proper resolution. If,
however, the element is already damaged (small fe ) or the elastic energy is small, the mesh resolution can be kept small
or even reduced for this element.
     During the damage evolution, there are only a few localized elements where the indicator value Ψe is large com-
pared to the bulk of elements with nonsignificant values. We therefore use the heuristic shown in Algorithm 3 for
the selection of elements, which should be refined or coarsened: All elements with values below the scaled average
  avg                                              avg
𝜃coarsen Ψavg are coarsened. Typical values of 𝜃coarsen are 0.1 or below, thus removing elements only if their deviation
from the average is really small. This is in particular the case for damaged elements once the damage process has fin-

ished locally. All elements with values above the scaled average 𝜃refine Ψavg are refined, using typical values for 𝜃refine
around 1.0. This leads to a detection of all elements that should be refined since they are more likely to damage than the
average.
     Upon mesh adaption, the relevant history data, that is, the damage, have to be transferred to the new mesh.
For mesh refinement, the damage value of the parent element is used for all child elements. For mesh coars-
ening, the coarse element is assigned the averaged value of all the removed children since all children have the
same element volume. For the displacements, we employ first-order Lagrangian shape functions. The arising hang-
ing nodes due to mesh adaptivity are handled by interpolation constraints enforcing the continuity over element
boundaries.

chosen brittle damage model. Using sufficiently fine load stepping and mesh refinement, we can accurately compute the
position of the force drop in a tensile test. The sensitivity with respect to the numerical parameter has been confirmed to
be below reasonable bounds. The remaining unstudied parameters are the dissipation parameter r, which we keep fixed
in this contribution, and the regularization parameter 𝛽 of the brittle material model. In Figure 10A, we have computed
the force-displacement curves for different choices of 𝛽 and different mesh refinement levels. For large regularization
values, 𝛽 = 10, 1 MPa mm2 , a convergence of the curves is observed. For 𝛽 = 0.1 MPa mm2 , level 3 is still not sufficient
and convergence is observed starting at level 4. For 𝛽 = 0.01, 0.001 MPa mm2 , a convergence of the curve is not observed.
Taking a look at the corresponding damage functions at crack initialization depicted in Figure 10B, this is in agreement
with the observation that the crack width has to be resolved by more than one element. With decreasing regularization
parameter 𝛽, the gradient of the damage function is less penalized and therefore the crack width decreases. Thus, more
refined meshes have to be used for smaller regularization parameters. For the non-converging cases, the crack is resolved
only by a single line of elements which is obviously too coarse.

a later displacement as can be observed in Figure 11B.

real-world specimen due to imperfections and local fluctuations.
    In Figure 20, we present the behavior of the model employing arc length control. For this study, an implementation
of the model in the finite element code FEAP42 has been used without adaptive load stepping and mesh adaptation. The
curve length is resolved using two different step counts such that the arc length increment differs by a factor of two. The
results show that there exist no remarkable differences in the curves and the structural toughness for the different arc
length increments. Furthermore, they reveal that an asymmetric distribution for the damage function is obtained even
for a symmetric finite element mesh. Although damage initialization is detected at both notches, only at the upper notch,
a distinct crack evolves. This behavior is accompanied by a snapback in the force-displacement diagram (Figure 20). After
reaching this maximum value of the reaction force, a rapid reduction both in force and displacement can be observed
which is almost parallel to the ascending loading. The force-displacement diagram shows that the dissipated energy,
which equals the area of the hysteresis, is much smaller than the apparent dissipated energy in our previous results, in
which the dissipated energy equals approximately the area of a triangle. This is due to the displacement-controlled bound-
ary conditions. This suggests employing arc length control with the presented adaptive meshing and loading strategies in
future works.

5        CO N C LUSION S AN D OU T LOOK

We presented a sophisticated study for the convergence properties of a gradient-enhanced brittle material model employ-
ing adaptive numerical strategies for load stepping and mesh handling, and analyzed the influence of the numerical
parameters on the solution accuracy. We found that adaptive strategies for load stepping and mesh refinement are advan-
tageous in terms of runtime and memory consumption while maintaining the desired degree of accuracy compared to
uniformly resolved approaches. This allows us to perform highly accurate simulations, which accurately resolve the force
drop in a tensile test, and we thus have a numerical tool at hand, which efficiently and accurately computes the crack
load for a given geometry and chosen model parameters. Therefore, we were able to study the influence of the regular-
ization parameter on the solution accuracy without interference with other numerical artifacts and found a very large
dependency of the displacement at which crack initiation occurs on the regularization parameter.
    The present contribution opens the door for a couple of subsequent works: first, we want to compare our numerical
predictivity to experiments. To this end, the dissipation parameter r has to be calibrated for a prescribed regularization
parameter 𝛽 and should be predictive for several geometry types. Second, an a priori fixed maximum level of refinement
is not purposeful. Instead, the local mesh resolution should be adapted based on the chosen regularization parameter. A
good heuristic for the fracture width may allow for a mesh refinement criterion solely based on 𝛽 and the local mechanical
energy, and thereby render the parameter Lmax obsolete. This is part of our ongoing work. Third, the imposed loading
step bounds dû min and dû max require an educated a priori guess. Ideally, the loading stepsize should solely depend on the
required accuracy and therefore only be controlled by the 𝜀load parameter or similar error measures. Fourth, the iteration
counts for the damage update increase quite drastically under mesh refinement. This is still acceptable due to the very
short execution time for one single iterate. However, it must be carefully taken into account for very large geometries or

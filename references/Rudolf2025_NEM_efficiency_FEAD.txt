1. Introduction

     In many problems of continuum mechanics, additional degrees of freedom are introduced to describe the behavior of various
multi-physical problems for regularized formulations. Examples can be found in topology optimization, damage mechanics [1–8],
phase transformation and bio-mechanical problems [9–11]. Regularization of the additional degrees of freedom is often unavoidable
due to the ill-posed mathematical fundamental problem resulting in numerical issues such as mesh dependency or checker-
boarding [12,13]. Overviews of numerical issues can be found for checker-boarding in [14,15] and for mesh dependency in [16–18].
Non-local regularization schemes are commonly used in all of those fields. Penalizing the highest gradient of the internal variable,
i.e., gradient-enhanced regularization of the free energy, is well established for this purpose [19–21]. It renders the formerly ill-posed
problem into an inherently well-posed one [12]. Due to the insertion of the Laplacian due to the regularization, the emerging partial
differential equation is similar to the standard heat or diffusion equation. The number of unknowns increases because not only the
displacements have to be evaluated, but also the equations for the additional internal variables. Consequently, the numerical cost
increases [12,13]. To ensure increasing complexity of the models which describe the underlying mechanisms, it is essential to develop
new numerical treatments, i.e., spatial discretizations that can reduce the time required for simulations while also minimizing the
amount of memory space.
     In previous publications, an efficient numerical treatment, the so called Neighbored Element Method (NEM), was developed and
applied to damage models [12,13,22] and topology optimization [20,23,24]. It was adapted from meshless methods, such as the
Diffuse Element Method (DEM), where the least-square approximation is implemented into a Galerkin framework, first introduced
by [25–27] and more refined into the element-free Galerkin (EFG) by [28]. Both, small strain and finite strain topology optimization
was performed using the NEM formulation. The NEM decouples the calculation of displacements and internal variables and solves
the system of differential equations with an operator split in a fully explicitly staggered approach. The displacements are calculated
in a standard Finite Element Method (FEM) formulation [29] or similar numerical schemes as for example the Virtual Element
Method [30]. The displacements are then used to solve the strong form of the evolution equation for the internal variables. For the
aforementioned regularization, the Laplacian of the internal variable needs to be computed. For structured meshes, it is obtained by
using a Finite Difference Method (FDM). For unstructured meshes, an inverted Taylor series expansion of the order two is utilized
where the first and second derivatives (mixed and unmixed) become the unknowns of a linear system of equations. This generalized
Finite Difference Method for arbitrary meshes was presented in [31–33]. To avoid a singular coefficient matrix, a weighted least-
square (WLS) is applied where more than the necessary nine neighbors in 3D (five in 2D) are taken into account. Application of the
Taylor series expansion with the weighted least-square approach in FEM context can be found in [26,34]. For further information,
the reader is referred to [23].
     The NEM is an efficient and robust numerical treatment. It conserves the size of the global system of equation, or the primal
unknowns, i.e., the displacements for the application of FEM, without internal variables. The update of the internal variable(s)
is computed separately, i.e., in a staggered manner, and has only minimal extra computational costs. The goal of this article is
to compare the NEM with a standard FEM formulation. For this, the commonly used ANSYS© Academic Research Mechanical,
Release 2022R1 software has been chosen. In principle, every differential equation with a spatial gradient could have been used
as a reference. Here, the built-in elements for thermo-mechanically and chemo-thermo-mechanically coupled materials serve as an
example. These built-in elements are chosen, since the ANSYS© code for those differential equations is efficiently implemented.
     The article is structured as follows. The methodology and mathematical derivation for the approximation of the Laplacian as well
as the solution scheme is described in Section 2. Section 3 presents the models for accuracy and efficiency comparison. Numerical
results are shown in Section 4, with the comparison of accuracy in Section 4.1 and efficiency in Section 4.2. In Section 4.3, the
influence of the numerical parameter is investigated. Finally, in Section 5, conclusions are drawn and possible future work is
mentioned.

2.1. NEM-scheme

    The general procedure of NEM is shown in Fig. 1 where the squared rectangles describe the calculation with FEM and the rounded
rectangles the calculation of the generalized FDM, i.e., the inverse Taylor series expansion. The inverted Taylor series expansion
of order two is applied, wherein the first and second derivatives are treated as the unknowns of a linear system of equations. It is
further explained in Section 2.2. Prior to solving a specific boundary value problem, the coefficient matrix 𝐃 has to be calculated
once for a given spatial distribution of the neighbored points, i.e., once for a given mesh if no remeshing is employed. The coefficient
matrix 𝐃 contains information about relative positioning of neighboring points. More elaboration on its derivation is provided in
Section 2.2. A change in initial conditions does not result in a recalculation of the coefficient matrix 𝐃. Except for the first time
step, 𝝊 is determined based on the current stress 𝝈 𝑡 . The resulting right-hand side of Eq. (9) can be used to calculate the gradient
or Laplacian of the internal variables. In the last step, the stresses are updated through a Newton–Raphson-iteration step. After
converging, we proceed to the subsequent time step.

2.2. Numerical treatment - approximation of the Laplacian

    The proposed scheme builds upon the procedure of the inversion of a Taylor series expansion and combination with the weighted
least-square method (WLS) to calculate function values and spatial derivatives for arbitrary point clouds [12,13,20,23,35] and to
compute the Laplace operator. The methods presented in previous studies, such as those referenced in [12,35], are subject to
limitations. This is due to the difficulty, or even impossibility, of identifying suitable neighbor points in each cardinal direction
which is dependent on the selected element type for spatial discretization or the finite element mesh. An inappropriate selection
of neighbor points may result in an unsuccessful calculation. This flaw can be resolved by applying more neighbors to the Taylor
series and therefore derive an over-determined equation system. This equation system can be solved with a weighted least-square
approach. The method is presented in [23] and recalled here for convenience. The Taylor series is defined by a reference point

3. Simulation model used for comparison

    The ANSYS© software is used in this work. This renowned program provides the ability to alter and modify established
procedures as needed. This can be accomplished using the APDL ANSYS© Parametric Design Language or user subroutines built
on the Fortran platform. Here, APDL is employed. It can be simply integrated into the ANSYS© user interface and provides
all necessary functionalities. Two key perspectives are examined in this analysis. First, a comparison of the results’ accuracy is
investigated. A quarter-torus model including the thermo-mechanical coupling as an example equation is employed for this purpose.
Then, a comparison is made between the NEM implementation and the traditional FEM. The second factor is efficiency. To be
more specific, we focus on the resources Random-Access-Memory (RAM) and the processing time. We investigate the performance
based on two exemplary coupled problems: the first one of these is the thermo-mechanical coupling, and the second one is the
chemo-thermo-mechanical coupling.

3.1. Model for the evaluation of the accuracy of the NEM

    To evaluate whether the NEM is an accurate scheme for the numerical solution of the thermo-mechanical problems, which serves
as an example equation system, a comparison with standard FEM is necessary. A quarter torus is selected as the reference geometry
due to its inherent complexity, characterized by the coexistence of two distinct curvatures. The inner radius is 𝑟 = 0.5 mm and the
outer radius is 𝑅 = 2 mm. The whole model with boundary conditions and mesh are shown in Fig. 3(a) and Fig. 3(b), respectively.
The material properties chosen are the standard ANSYS© steel properties. They are listed in Table 1.
    The calculation is a coupled field simulation comprising three DOF’s for displacements and one additional DOF for the additional
field variable, which is employed here as an illustrative example of temperature. It is assumed that no heat flow can occur over the
boundary surfaces, i.e., perfect isolation. Hence, the Neumann boundaries conditions Eq. (11) are implemented in NEM to ensure
perfect isolation. Furthermore, the red arrow labeled with BC indicates the plane surface for the non-zero boundary condition. The
temperature of the surface remains constant at 𝜃0 = 1 ◦ C throughout the duration of the simulation. All nodes on the surface BC are
defined as fixed supports of displacements. The initial temperature of all other nodes is given by 𝜃 = 0 ◦ C. Furthermore, the time
step size, 𝛥𝑡 = 2 × 10−5 s, is employed. The final time point, 𝑡 = 0.1 s, is reached after 5000 time steps.
    The meshing algorithm from ANSYS© software with standard elements was applied. Both, regular and irregular meshes were
investigated in this model. The regular mesh is created by hexahedral elements SOLID185 with linear shape functions. The nominal
edge length of these elements is ℎ = 0.05 mm which results in 34965 elements and 37260 nodes, respectively. To generate
the irregular mesh, tetrahedral elements SOLID185 with linear shape function are applied. Furthermore, additional information
regarding the specific element type can be found in the ANSYS© manual [37]. This results in 68819 elements and 14728 nodes,
respectively. In addition, the Neumann-stability criterium in Eq. (3) is satisfied by the given material properties, the time step size
and the mesh size.
    The same meshes are used in the FEM and NEM implementation in ANSYS© . This allows a direct comparison between the nodal
values. The ANSYS© simulation serves as benchmark for these results. The point P1 in Fig. 3 indicates the node position at which
the values of the results will be compared. This node is located inside of the body, surrounded by neighboring nodes. The dotted
magenta line indicates the position of cross-section surface for further visual comparison.

   To investigate the efficiency of the NEM, the thermo-mechanical and chemo-thermo-mechanical problem were solved. The results
were compared to results obtained with built-in elements in ANSYS© and evaluated using two criteria. The first one is the comparison

4. Results

4.1. Evaluation of the accuracy of the NEM


   The evolution of the temperature, through the torus with hexahedral elements by using the NEM is demonstrated in Fig. 5.
The results are in line with an expected behavior. As conduction through the torus progresses, the temperature increases gradually
through the domain. The propagation of the contour front shows that the system tends to reach the steady-state determined by
the uniform boundary value of unity. Here, the calculation was carried out until all values have reached a significant difference
from the initial temperature which is in this case at about 𝑡 = 0.1 s. As illustrated in Fig. 3(a), the boundary surface labeled BC
demonstrates zero deformation across all nodes. Conversely, the remaining nodes display a progressive expansion in conjunction
with the diffusion of temperature throughout the body which is indicative of the thermoelasticity coupling phenomenon.
   As local error measurement, the relative difference between the ANSYS© and the NEM solution is computed for each node of
the mesh. It is normalized by the ANSYS© solution, i.e.

     The results on the cross-section surface for the hexahedral and tetrahedral meshes are presented in Fig. 6. The maximum error

error is reduced to 0.003 %. The tetrahedral mesh also provides a clear visual representation. This suggests that the position of the

error than the tetrahedral mesh on this cross section surface.
     The investigation of the maximum relative error as well as the average error over the entire torus is presented in Fig. 7. Data
is taken every 𝑡 = 0.001 s. The image provides a clear interpretation of the error evolution in time. With ongoing time, the error
asymptotically approaches to zero while the diffusion is still in progress. The maximum error of the tetrahedral mesh is higher
than the error of the hexahedral mesh. The accuracy of the method is reliant upon the equiangularity of the distribution of the
neighboring points around the reference point. An almost equiangular distribution is exhibited by a structured mesh and therefore,
the numerical error is smaller for the hexahedral mesh. This behavior was also presented in the previous example, c.f. Fig. 6.
Initially, the maximum error exceeds 1.5 % and 5.5 %, respectively. For the hexahedral mesh, the error was reduced to below 1 %
at 𝑡 = 0.002 s, while for the tetrahedral mesh, this was achieved at 𝑡 = 0.005 s. The initial average value is below 1 %. It falls below
0.2 % at 𝑡 = 0.005 s for the hexahedral mesh and at 𝑡 = 0.003 s for the tetrahedral mesh.
     The convergence of the maximum relative error is characterized by both stagnation and jumps. For instance, at 𝑡 = 0.013 s the
tetrahedral mesh exhibits a jump. In each instance of the aforementioned jump, the location of the maximum error shifts from
one node to another, resulting in a different denominator in Eq. (22) and, consequently, a sudden alteration in the local error
measurement. Conversely, the investigation of the absolute error results in convergence, indicating that the calculation is accurate.
Therefore, this jump occurs due to the high change in the basis value for the relative error. In Fig. 7, the error between NEM and
ANSYS© decreases over time.

to investigate the convergence of the solution with respect to the mesh size, four different mesh sizes were utilized at 𝑡 = 0.005 s,
i.e., 0.02 mm, 0.035 mm, 0.05 mm and 0.065 mm. It can be observed that the solution converges with a finer mesh. The maximum error

5. Conclusion and outlook

   This work presents a comparative study between the standard FEM approach and the Neighbored Element Method (NEM)
evaluated in ANSYS© . The combination of FEM and the inverse Taylor series expansion with the weighted least-square method
in NEM was evaluated through the comparison of two distinct models. The accuracy and resource requirements of this method were
investigated. The results of the accuracy assessment are presented as a relative error which has been normalized in relation to the
ANSYS© solution. The results indicate a linear convergence in spatial discretization and a convergent solution in time progression.
There is no mesh dependency, and the method is applicable to hexahedral and tetrahedral meshes. The computational resources
comparison demonstrates a clear saving of calculation time and RAM by orders of magnitude. The spatial discretization by using
the NEM can be different to the FEM which creates more options for resolving coupled problems.
   While the results demonstrate that the NEM significantly reduces computation time and memory consumption, it must be
considered that this is partly due to the choice of a staggered approach. A comparison with a commercial staggered implementation
would be necessary to further quantify the specific advantages of the NEM. This is left for further investigations. In future works,
the influence of the value of the weighting function and meshless methods or hybrid meshes, i.e., meshes made from hexahedral
as well as tetrahedral elements, can be investigated further. Moreover, the NEM can be applied to a number of additional research
topics, including phase transformation, as well as fatigue and biological modeling.
